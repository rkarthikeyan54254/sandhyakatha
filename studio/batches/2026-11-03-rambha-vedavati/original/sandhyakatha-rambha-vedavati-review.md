# Rambhā and the Sevenfold Curse

**Tease:** She had named the man she loved, asked to be released, and refused. Rāvaṇa ignored her. The curse came afterward.

**Source:** Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Uttara Kāṇḍa, 7.26.20–59 (common printed numbering); especially 7.26.28–46 and 7.26.56–59.

**Ages:** 15+ | **Gated:** Yes | **Status:** in-review

**Before you begin (for the parent):** For the parent: Rāvaṇa ignores Rambhā’s refusal and rapes her. There is no graphic description. Rambhā later asks pardon although she was the victim; the story makes clear that she did nothing wrong. Nalakūbara curses Rāvaṇa that his head will split in seven if he forces another unwilling woman. This is not a routine bedtime pick.

**Tradition note:** This telling follows Uttara Kāṇḍa 26 (common printed numbering). The related Yuddha Kāṇḍa 13 account tells of a different assault on Puñjikasthalā and a curse by Brahmā involving a hundred pieces, not seven; the episodes are not combined. Critical-edition verse numbers differ. Stability across all recensions has not been independently collated.

## Six-minute telling

The army of «Rāvaṇa» had halted on «Kailāsa». The sun had gone down, and the soldiers slept while the moon lit the mountain forests.

There were flowering trees, pools of lotuses, and the waters of the «Mandākinī». Music carried from the singers and dancers who lived among the mountains.

«Rāvaṇa» sat above the camp. Then he saw an apsarā passing through the sleeping army, wearing fresh flowers and ornaments for a meeting she had chosen.

Her name was «Rambhā». She was on her way to «Nalakūbara», whom she loved and who was waiting for her.

*— pause —*

«Rāvaṇa» rose and stopped her. He asked where she was going, praised her beauty, and urged her to remain with him instead.

«Rambhā» was frightened. She folded her hands and appealed to the duty he owed her: _You should protect me from violence, not threaten me with it._

She told him he was an elder in her family. «Nalakūbara» was the son of «Kubera», and «Kubera» was «Rāvaṇa»'s brother. By that relationship, «Rambhā» was his daughter-in-law.

«Rāvaṇa» questioned her claim. She explained it again, naming the man she had come to meet and saying that her heart was set on him alone.

«Nalakūbara» was waiting. She had dressed for him; she had given her word to him. She asked «Rāvaṇa» to let her go and to follow the conduct expected of a guardian of his family.

*— pause —*

«Rāvaṇa» would not accept her refusal. He argued that apsarās did not follow the same rules of marriage as women who had only one husband.

But «Rambhā» had not come to him. She had asked to leave. She had explained whose company she wanted, and it was not his.

He ignored her pleas and raped her. The account does not hide what he did, and neither should we mistake his arguments for permission.

When she could leave, «Rambhā» went to «Nalakūbara». Her flowers and ornaments were disturbed. She was shaking.

«Nalakūbara» asked why she had come to him in such distress. «Rambhā» told him how «Rāvaṇa» had stopped her, questioned her, and refused to listen even after she spoke of their family relationship.

She told him that «Rāvaṇa» had overpowered her. She even asked «Nalakūbara» to pardon her, though the wrong had been done to her, not by her.

She had first appealed to his duty as an elder. Then she had explained their kinship. Then she had spoken of the man waiting for her.

Each appeal had been clear; none had stopped «Rāvaṇa».

*— pause —*

«Nalakūbara» heard her account. He entered meditation and established what had happened. He became angry at the violence committed against her.

Taking water in his hands, he spoke a curse against «Rāvaṇa»: _If you ever again force yourself on a woman who does not want you, your head will break into seven pieces._

It was not a punishment placed upon «Rambhā». The curse fell upon the man who had refused to respect her will.

The tale says that divine drums sounded and flowers fell from the sky when the curse was pronounced. Those who understood what lay ahead knew that «Rāvaṇa» had been checked.

When the warning reached «Rāvaṇa», he was shaken. The text says he thereafter ceased to force sexual union upon women who did not desire him.

Women whom he had already carried away against their wishes heard of the curse. They rejoiced at the protection it promised them.

*— pause —*

Long afterward, in «Laṅkā», «Rāvaṇa» would hold «Sītā» captive and demand that she accept him. She would not. There was still a distance between having power over a captive and winning her willing consent.

For this night on «Kailāsa», the story remains «Rambhā»'s: she refused him, she told the truth of what followed, and the consequence was placed where it belonged.

> The mountain had heard «Rāvaṇa» speak as though power gave him a right. «Rambhā» had said no. It was his deed, not her refusal, that brought the curse.

## Three-minute telling

On a moonlit night, the army of «Rāvaṇa» rested on «Kailāsa». Among the flowers and music of the mountain, an apsarā named «Rambhā» passed the sleeping soldiers.

She was going to meet «Nalakūbara», whom she loved. «Rāvaṇa» saw her, stopped her, and asked her to stay with him instead.

«Rambhā» refused. She told him that «Nalakūbara» was the son of his brother «Kubera»; by their family relationship she was «Rāvaṇa»'s daughter-in-law.

She asked him to protect her, as an elder should, and let her go. The man she had chosen was waiting for her.

*— pause —*

«Rāvaṇa» argued that apsarās need not be bound to one partner. He paid no attention to the plain fact that «Rambhā» did not want him.

He overpowered and raped her. Her refusal had been clear, and nothing about his position gave him permission.

«Rambhā» had tried more than one way to be heard. She had asked for protection, explained their family connection, and told him who was waiting for her. He refused to respect any of it.

Shaken, «Rambhā» went to «Nalakūbara». She told him exactly what had happened. She even asked his pardon, although the wrong was not hers.

*— pause —*

«Nalakūbara» listened, entered meditation, and understood the truth. He took water in his hands and pronounced a curse upon «Rāvaṇa».

_If you ever again force yourself upon a woman who does not want you, your head will split into seven pieces._

The curse was not against «Rambhā». It was against the one who had harmed her.

The gods rejoiced. When «Rāvaṇa» learned what had been said, he was afraid, and the text says he no longer forced sexual union on unwilling women.

Other women he had carried away rejoiced when they heard this. The curse gave them a protection that his power had denied them.

> «Rambhā» had said no before the assault. She should not have needed a curse to make that answer matter.

## Now turn to your child

Have you ever told someone to stop, or heard someone else say stop? What do you think listening should look like in that moment?

*If they shrug:* A refusal is enough; the person who ignores it is responsible for the harm.

## If they ask

**Why did Rambhā ask Nalakūbara to pardon her?**

The text says she was frightened and sought his pardon after telling him what had happened. She had not chosen the assault. Asking pardon here is part of her distress, not proof that she was responsible. Nalakūbara directs the curse at Rāvaṇa, not at her.

**What does it mean that Rāvaṇa raped her?**

It means he forced sexual contact on Rambhā when she did not want it. She had asked him to let her go. No relationship, rank, or argument about customs can turn that refusal into permission. The text names this violence without needing a graphic description.

**Is this the curse that protected Sītā?**

Uttara Kāṇḍa 26 tells of Nalakūbara’s seven-piece curse after the assault on Rambhā. Yuddha Kāṇḍa 13 separately has Rāvaṇa describe Brahmā’s hundred-piece curse after he assaulted Puñjikasthalā, and he says that curse kept him from forcing Sītā. We should keep the two accounts distinct.

# Vedavatī and the Vow That Returned

**Tease:** In a forest, she refused Rāvaṇa and defended her vow. When he seized her hair, she made a promise the Rāmāyaṇa would remember.

**Source:** Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Uttara Kāṇḍa, 7.17.1–44 (common printed numbering); especially 7.17.27–34 and the concluding Sītā identification.

**Ages:** 14+ | **Gated:** Yes | **Status:** in-review

**Before you begin (for the parent):** For the parent: Rāvaṇa pressures Vedavatī after she refuses him and seizes her by the hair. She cuts off the hair he holds, prepares a fire, and enters it, ending her life. Her father was murdered and her mother also entered a fire. The story names these events but does not depict them graphically. Do not present self-harm as a response children should imitate, or suggest that a victim becomes impure through an assault. Parent opt-in recommended.

**Tradition note:** Follows the Uttara Kāṇḍa 17 account of Vedavatī and her identification with Sītā; the same identification is recalled by Rāvaṇa in Yuddha Kāṇḍa 6.60.10. The hair-grabbing scene is assault, not completed rape. Verse numbering varies between editions. Stability across all recensions has not been independently collated.

## Six-minute telling

In a forest near the «Himālayas», «Vedavatī» lived as an ascetic. She wore a black antelope skin and kept her hair in matted locks.

She had not come there by accident. Her father, the sage «Kuśadhvaja», had devoted himself to the «Vedas» and wished that his daughter should be united with «Viṣṇu».

Many powerful beings had once sought her hand. Her father refused those proposals because he had made his choice for «Viṣṇu».

A proud daitya named «Śambhu» was enraged by the refusal. He killed «Kuśadhvaja» while the sage slept. «Vedavatī»'s mother then entered the funeral fire holding her husband.

«Vedavatī» continued her own vow. Her heart, she said, was given to «Nārāyaṇa», another name for «Viṣṇu», and she undertook severe tapas for that purpose.

*— pause —*

One day «Rāvaṇa» entered the forest. He saw the young ascetic and wondered why someone of her age and beauty was living with such austerity.

He asked whose daughter she was and why she had chosen this life. «Vedavatī» received him with the hospitality due to a visitor before explaining her family and her vow.

She told him about her father, the marriage proposals, the murder, and the penance she now performed. She also told him she knew who he was.

Then she asked «Rāvaṇa» to leave. Her purpose was not hidden and her decision was not open for him to make.

But «Rāvaṇa» stepped down from his chariot. He called himself the ruler of «Laṅkā», offered wealth and pleasure, and told her to become his consort.

He also spoke contemptuously of «Viṣṇu», claiming to be greater in power. He treated her chosen devotion as something he was entitled to overturn.

«Vedavatī» would not accept those words. She defended «Viṣṇu» and rebuked «Rāvaṇa» for speaking against the Lord honoured by all the worlds.

*— pause —*

Then «Rāvaṇa» seized her by the hair. This was the assault the text describes: an unwanted touch after a clear refusal, not a completed rape.

«Vedavatī» was furious. With her own hand, which took on the sharpness of a sword, she cut off the hair he had seized.

She did not turn to him for permission to be free. She cut away what he held and spoke of the injury he had done.

What followed is difficult. «Vedavatī» said that after his violation she would not continue living in that body. She prepared a fire and resolved to enter it before his eyes.

She told «Rāvaṇa» that because he had insulted and assaulted her in the forest, she would be born again for his destruction.

She would not spend the power of her tapas on a curse there and then. Instead she prayed that the good she had done through gifts, sacrifice, and austerity would allow her a new and worthy birth.

*— pause —*

«Vedavatī» entered the fire she had kindled. The account says that flowers fell from above.

The telling does not ask us to pretend this ending is easy. It says plainly that she ended her life in the fire, and it preserves the vow she made before doing so.

The sage who recounts the episode then tells «Rāma» who «Vedavatī» became: she was born again as «Sītā», the daughter of «Janaka».

«Janaka» found «Sītā» as he ploughed a field. In the narrative, this birth was the answer to «Vedavatī»'s declared intention to return and become the cause of «Rāvaṇa»'s fall.

Later, when «Rāvaṇa» suffered defeat at the hands of «Rāma», he himself remembered «Vedavatī». In the «Yuddha Kāṇḍa», he spoke of the wrong he had done her and recognised «Sītā» as her return.

*— pause —*

The tale names «Vedavatī» as the woman who returned, not as someone forgotten in the forest. Its next turn belongs to «Sītā», who is later taken to «Laṅkā» by the same «Rāvaṇa».

> «Rāvaṇa» thought he had interrupted a vow in a lonely forest. The Rāmāyaṇa carries that vow forward into the life of «Sītā» and the fall of «Rāvaṇa».

## Three-minute telling

In the «Himālayas», a young ascetic named «Vedavatī» practised tapas. She had devoted herself to «Nārāyaṇa», or «Viṣṇu», and would accept no other husband.

Her father, the sage «Kuśadhvaja», had wished the same. Many had asked to marry her, but he had refused them. A daitya named «Śambhu» killed him after that refusal; her mother entered the funeral fire.

Still «Vedavatī» continued her vow. One day, as she lived in the forest, «Rāvaṇa» arrived and asked why she had chosen such a hard life.

She welcomed him as a visitor, told him who she was, explained her devotion to «Viṣṇu», and asked him to go.

«Rāvaṇa» would not listen. He offered her life beside him as ruler of «Laṅkā» and spoke proudly against «Viṣṇu».

«Vedavatī» rejected his words. She had made her choice, and neither his power nor his offers changed it.

*— pause —*

Then «Rāvaṇa» seized her hair. Angry at being assaulted, she cut off the hair he held. Her hand became sharp as a sword.

What happened next is painful: «Vedavatī» prepared a fire and chose to enter it. Before she did, she told «Rāvaṇa» that she would return in another birth to bring about his destruction.

She would not use up her tapas by cursing him at once. Instead, she asked that her gifts, sacrifices and austerities lead to a worthy new birth.

Then she entered the flames. Flowers fell from above.

*— pause —*

The Rāmāyaṇa tells us the next part. «Vedavatī» was born again as «Sītā», whom «Janaka» found in a ploughed field.

Years later, in «Laṅkā», «Rāvaṇa» himself remembered the wrong he had done «Vedavatī» and recognised that «Sītā» had come into the world. He spoke of her after returning from battle against «Rāma».

> «Rāvaṇa» had tried to break «Vedavatī»'s decision. Her vow remained part of the story long after he left that forest.

## Now turn to your child

Have you ever held on to a promise after someone tried to change your mind? What helped you decide what was yours to choose?

*If they shrug:* No amount of power gave Rāvaṇa the right to make Vedavatī’s choice for her.

## If they ask

**Did Rāvaṇa rape Vedavatī?**

In Uttara Kāṇḍa 17 he tries to persuade her to become his consort and, after her refusal, seizes her hair. The passage does not describe completed rape. Grabbing her hair against her will is itself a violent assault.

**Why did Vedavatī enter the fire? Is that what someone should do after being assaulted?**

The Rāmāyaṇa says she chose to enter the fire after Rāvaṇa seized her hair, and that she vowed to be reborn for his destruction. This is a grave and troubling part of this specific sacred narrative, not advice for anyone who has been harmed. A person who has been assaulted is not impure and does not owe anyone death or punishment. In our lives, they deserve safety, support and care.

**Was Vedavatī really Sītā?**

The Uttara Kāṇḍa explicitly identifies Vedavatī’s next birth as Sītā, found by Janaka in a ploughed field. Rāvaṇa also recalls that connection in Yuddha Kāṇḍa 6.60.10. The telling follows those passages without adding details from other retellings.

Pārvatī and the Visitor Who Questioned Her Choice

An aged ascetic asks why Pārvatī seeks Śiva. When his questions turn into criticism, she tells him what she sees in Śiva—and discovers who has come to visit.

Parent care note

The complete source scene includes Pārvatī's earlier account of entering a fire that becomes cool, and later contains severe words against those who disparage Śiva. This telling begins with the visitor's questions and does not narrate the fire or repeat the severe threats aloud.

Short telling · 304 spoken words · timing pending

Pārvatī had come to the forest to perform tapas, a long and demanding practice. She wished to marry Śiva. One day an aged ascetic arrived, carrying a staff and an umbrella.

Pārvatī welcomed him with care, as she would welcome any guest who came to the forest. The visitor asked why she was undertaking such difficult tapas. Her friend told him whom Pārvatī hoped to marry, and Pārvatī confirmed it herself.

The ascetic began to question her choice. He described Śiva's matted hair, the ash on his body, the snakes around him and the bull he rode. Why, he asked, would the daughter of a mountain king choose someone so unlike the husband people expected for her?

· · ·

Pārvatī listened, then answered. The visitor could see the ash and the snakes, she said, but he had not understood the one who wore them. Śiva was not measured by fine clothes, wealth or a family name.

She had not begun her tapas because she had failed to notice how Śiva appeared. She knew the form he took and still wished to reach him. Even when the visitor spoke of his ash as something unclean, Pārvatī asked why the gods themselves honoured it.

The ascetic tried to speak again. Pārvatī turned to her friend: she did not want to hear more words against Śiva. She was ready to leave, keeping the choice she had made.

· · ·

Before she could go, the visitor changed. The aged ascetic was Śiva himself, come in disguise to see and test her. Now he stood before her in the form she had longed to see.

Śiva asked her to forgive the test and spoke of their bond. Pārvatī rejoiced. She had seen past the visitor's arguments to the person she had sought all along—and the one she sought had come to meet her.

Full telling · 651 spoken words · timing pending

Pārvatī had left her father's house and come to a forest for tapas. She wished to marry Śiva, and she had given herself to a long, demanding practice to reach him. Her friend stayed beside her in that forest.

One day a visitor came to the place where she was practising. He looked like an aged ascetic, with matted hair, a staff and an umbrella. Pārvatī received him respectfully and asked after his health, as she would for a guest who had travelled far.

The visitor asked questions in return. Who was she, and why would someone so young choose this hard life in the forest? He praised the strength of her tapas, yet kept asking what she wanted from it.

Pārvatī told him that she was the mountain king's daughter. When he pressed her about her wish, she asked her friend to explain. Her friend said plainly that Pārvatī wanted no husband but Śiva, however long her tapas might take.

· · ·

The visitor asked Pārvatī to say it herself. She had sought Śiva with her mind, her words and her actions, she told him. Reaching him was difficult, but that was why she continued her tapas.

The ascetic seemed ready to leave after hearing this. Pārvatī stopped him and asked him to give his advice. He said that if she truly wished to listen, she should consider the man she had chosen.

He began with what anyone could see. Śiva wore ash and had matted hair; snakes twined around him and a bull carried him. He wore skins instead of the splendid cloth expected in a royal home.

The visitor kept going. Śiva carried a skull and spent time with beings others feared, while no one could name his birth or family. Why, he asked, would the mountain king's daughter choose a life with him?

He compared ornaments to snakes, fine cloth to skins, and sandal paste to ash. Each comparison made the same point: by the measures of an ordinary household, Śiva seemed unlike the husband everyone might choose for her. The ascetic wanted Pārvatī to turn away.

· · ·

Pārvatī did not pretend that the ash or the snakes were not there. She had seen the form Śiva took. What she refused was the visitor's judgment that these outward signs told him all he needed to know.

She answered that Śiva could not be measured by a family name. He took a visible form, but was more than that form. The visitor had called the ash unclean; Pārvatī asked why even the gods wore ash from Śiva's body with honour.

She spoke of Śiva as the one who creates, sustains and ends worlds. A person counting clothes and ornaments might never understand him, she said. Those things were easy to see; the one she sought required another kind of seeing.

Pārvatī had come to the forest because she knew whom she wished to reach. The visitor's long list did not change that. She said that whatever form Śiva wore, he remained dear to her.

After speaking, Pārvatī became quiet and fixed her mind on Śiva. The ascetic began again. She told her friend that she would not hear more words against Śiva, and turned to leave.

· · ·

As she stepped away, the visitor stopped her.

The aged ascetic was Śiva. He let the disguise fall and appeared before her in the form she had held in her mind during tapas. All those questions had come from the one she was trying to reach.

Śiva said that he was pleased with her steadfastness. He acknowledged that he had tested her and asked her to forgive him for it. He told her that she was dear to him, and that they belonged together.

Pārvatī rejoiced. Her long practice had led to this meeting. The visitor had named every outward reason to turn away, but she had kept sight of Śiva as she knew him.

After the story

When have you understood something about a person that their clothes or appearance did not show?

An outward sign can be real and still tell only a small part of who someone is.

Why did Śiva speak against himself?

Śiva Purāṇa, Pārvatī-khaṇḍa 26.1–3 says he came in disguise to see Pārvatī and test her tapas. In 28.46 he acknowledges the test and asks forgiveness. The story leaves room to talk about whether that was fair to her.

Why did Pārvatī leave instead of listening longer?

She had already answered what the visitor said about Śiva. In Śiva Purāṇa, Pārvatī-khaṇḍa 28.33–40, he begins to criticize Śiva again, and Pārvatī tells her friend they should leave. Her choice is to stop hearing the disparagement; she does not have to remain in that exchange.

Why was Pārvatī angry?

She heard the ascetic repeatedly disparage Śiva. Chapter 28.33–40 gives her a strong response: she stops the exchange and chooses to leave rather than hear more. This telling keeps that action; it does not turn her anger into a lesson that she ought to be merely polite.

The Sacrifice Without an Invitation

Dakṣa invites the gods to a great sacrifice, but leaves out his daughter Satī and her husband Śiva.

Parent care note

Dakṣa publicly humiliates his daughter Satī and insults her husband. Satī deliberately ends her life through yogic fire. Śiva's followers destroy the sacrifice and Dakṣa is beheaded before being restored. Read this with an older child; her death is not a remedy for family conflict.

Short telling · 300 spoken words · timing pending

Dakṣa prepared a great sacrifice. Gods and sages came. Yet he sent no invitation to his daughter Satī or to her husband Śiva. One sage asked how the rite could be complete without them. Dakṣa refused to change his mind, and the sage left.

Satī learned where the others were going. This was her father's house, and she wanted to go. Śiva warned her that an uninvited visit could end in hurt. She went anyway, hoping that her father would welcome her.

She did not arrive quietly. She came with attendants to a hall full of people who knew her father. At a gathering where each person had a place, the missing place for her husband was plain to see.

· · ·

At the sacrifice she found no place set aside for Śiva. She asked Dakṣa why he had left her husband out. He answered by speaking against Śiva before the gathering. The insult came from her own father, in front of all those guests.

Satī answered him. She would not pretend his words were harmless. Then she deliberately gave up her life through yogic fire. The people at the sacrifice cried out. Her father could no longer take back what he had said.

· · ·

When Śiva heard, he was furious. From a lock of his hair he brought forth Vīrabhadra and sent him to the sacrifice. The gathering was thrown into violence. The rite was destroyed, and Dakṣa was killed.

Later Śiva came to the ruined place. He said the punishment had gone too far. He ordered Dakṣa restored to life with a goat's head. The father woke and bowed to Śiva.

The sacrifice could begin again. Satī could not come back to that hall. What her father had said there, and what it cost, stayed at the centre of the story.

Full telling · 670 spoken words · timing pending

Dakṣa called gods and sages to a great sacrifice. Invitations went out; places were prepared; the rite was ready to begin. But he did not call his daughter Satī or her husband Śiva. The absence was deliberate. An old quarrel with Śiva had settled in Dakṣa's heart.

At the gathering, a sage named Dadhīci looked around and asked where Śiva was. He said a sacrifice that left him out could not be whole. Dakṣa grew angry and would not send an invitation. Dadhīci walked away.

Far off, Satī saw others travelling to her father's sacrifice. She had not known it was being held. Her friend asked where they were going, then brought the answer back. Satī wondered why her own parents had not invited her.

The travellers included Candra and his companion Rohiṇī. Satī asked her friend Vijayā to find out their destination. The answer was her father's hall, full of guests and activity. She had to learn about it from someone passing by.

She went to Śiva and asked him to come with her. He knew the quarrel and warned her. A visit where they were not wanted, he said, could wound her. But Satī still wanted to go to the home where she had been a daughter.

Śiva spoke of the insult that might wait for them there. Satī heard him, but the silence from her family troubled her too. She went with attendants while he remained behind. Neither of them could make Dakṣa send the welcome that should have come freely.

· · ·

She arrived with attendants. The sacrifice was under way. Offerings had been prepared for the gods, yet there was no share for Śiva. The empty place made her father's refusal plain before she spoke to him.

Satī asked Dakṣa why Śiva had been excluded. She spoke with anger, but also with the claim of a daughter who expected her father to hear her. Others at the rite listened without answering.

Then Dakṣa answered. He told her she could stay or leave. Before the gathered guests he spoke contemptuously of her husband. He told her to calm down and smile, as if the words he had just said could be put aside.

Satī did not smile. She rejected what her father had said and the body she had received from him. In this Śiva Purāṇa telling, she sat in yogic concentration and deliberately ended her life in an inner fire. She did not leap into the sacrificial altar.

Those who saw her death cried out. Some of Śiva's attendants moved against the sacrifice and were driven back. The news went to Śiva; he heard the account from those who had been there.

· · ·

Śiva was overcome by anger. He pulled a lock from his matted hair and struck the mountain. From it came Vīrabhadra, a fierce leader. Śiva sent him with his attendants to bring down the sacrifice.

The place that had been arranged so carefully was broken. The account tells of a terrible battle: gods and attendants fought, the rite was scattered, and people were wounded. Vīrabhadra seized Dakṣa and took his head.

The anger did not bring Satī back. It left another family member dead and the sacrifice in ruins. The gods and sages went to Śiva and asked him to help set right what remained.

· · ·

Śiva came and looked at the damage. He told Vīrabhadra that the punishment had been too severe. When Dakṣa's body was brought, his head was gone. Śiva ordered that the head of a sacrificial goat be joined to him instead.

Dakṣa woke. He saw Śiva before him and bowed. The rite could be put in order again, now including the one whom Dakṣa had tried to exclude. The father who had refused an invitation had to face the person he had despised.

For the parent, not read aloud

For the parent: chapter 30 describes Satī's death through yogic fire; many familiar retellings describe a leap into the altar. This edition follows the cited Śiva Purāṇa passage. Do not frame Satī's death as needed for reconciliation or suggest that family contempt makes self-harm inevitable.

The gathering could make room for Śiva at last. Nothing could restore the moment before Satī stood before her father and heard what he thought of her life. That harm remains, even after the broken rite is repaired.

After the story

If you had been at that gathering, what could you have said when you saw Satī being hurt?

Silence can let a cruel word keep its place in a room.

Why did Satī end her life?

In this Śiva Purāṇa passage, she is devastated and angry at her father's public contempt for Śiva and rejects the body she received from him. The text presents a goddess's extraordinary act through yogic fire; it is not a safe or right response for a person facing family humiliation. If a person is hurting, they deserve help and company, not death.

Did she jump into the sacrificial fire?

Not in the Śiva Purāṇa passage we use. Chapter 30 says she sat in yogic concentration and her body fell in an inner yogic fire. Other tellings and images place her in the external altar; we do not blend those versions here.

Was Dakṣa forgiven?

The text says Śiva ordered Dakṣa restored with a goat's head and Dakṣa bowed to him. It also says Śiva rebuked the severity of the punishment. It does not show Satī returning to that hall. Restoring Dakṣa and the sacrifice does not erase what happened to her.

Kaṇṇappar Gives What He Has

A hunter brings water, flowers and food to a hill shrine. When one eye of the image begins to bleed, he tries to help.

Parent care note

A hunter takes out one of his own eyes when an image of Śiva bleeds and moves to give the other; Śiva stops him. The telling states the act briefly without describing how to do it. Read this yourself before sharing it with a child; it is a saint's extraordinary act, not a model for self-injury.

Short telling · 323 spoken words · timing pending

Tiṇṇaṉ was a hunter. One day he climbed the hill of Kāḷatti and found an image of Śiva there. He was moved to care for the Lord he had found.

He brought food from his hunt, flowers from the forest and water carried in his mouth. He had no vessel for the water. He gave what he could carry, then stayed near the shrine because he did not want the Lord to be alone.

A priest also served that shrine. He removed the hunter's offerings and performed the worship he had been taught. The two men came by different paths, and each thought he was caring for Śiva.

· · ·

Then the priest saw blood at one eye of the image. Śiva had told him in a dream to watch what the hunter would do. The priest stayed hidden. Tiṇṇaṉ came up the hill and saw the bleeding eye. His water and food fell from his hands.

He tried to wipe the blood away. He searched the hill for healing herbs and put their juice on the eye. Nothing stopped it. To Tiṇṇaṉ, the image was someone he loved who was hurt before him.

So he took out one of his own eyes and gave it to the bleeding eye of the image. The bleeding stopped. Tiṇṇaṉ was glad, until blood began to flow from the other eye.

· · ·

He still had one eye. He moved to give that one too. Before he could, Śiva reached out and stopped his hand. Stop, Kaṇṇappar, the Lord called to him, three times.

The priest had watched the whole thing. What he had thought unfit for worship had come from a hunter's fierce love. Śiva gave the hunter a place beside him.

Tiṇṇaṉ had brought what was in his hands: food, flowers, water. When he saw the bleeding eye, he forgot to ask what was proper to offer. He saw only someone he could not bear to leave hurting.

Full telling · 658 spoken words · timing pending

Tiṇṇaṉ grew up among hunters. He knew the paths through the forest, the work of a bow and the places where water ran down from the hills. On a hunt he followed a boar far from the rest of his party. By the time it stopped, he had come near the hill of Kāḷatti.

A companion told him that there was a shrine of Śiva on the hill. Tiṇṇaṉ climbed to it and saw the image. He stood before it for a long time. A deep love came over him, and he could hardly bring himself to leave.

He wondered who would look after the Lord if he went away. Yet he also thought someone must bring food. He went down the hill reluctantly, promising to return with something good to eat. The worry remained with him as he crossed the river.

He thought the Lord must be hungry. He went back for meat from the hunt and returned with it. He picked flowers too. For water, he had no vessel, so he carried it in his mouth up the hill. He offered everything to the image.

Tiṇṇaṉ did not know the shrine's order of worship. He moved the old flowers with his foot, placed his own flowers there and poured the water he had carried. His offering was the food and care he knew how to give.

· · ·

A priest served the same shrine. When he found the hunter's offerings, he cleared them away and performed worship according to the rules he knew. On another day Tiṇṇaṉ returned, and then the priest returned. Neither man could see the whole of the other's care.

The priest grieved over what he found. Then, in a dream, Śiva spoke to him about the hunter. The Lord told him to watch when Tiṇṇaṉ came again. At dawn the priest went to the shrine and waited out of sight.

Before Tiṇṇaṉ arrived, the priest saw blood flow from one eye of the image. He stayed where he was. The hunter climbed the hill with flowers, water, food and his bow. He saw the blood. The things he carried fell as he rushed forward.

Tiṇṇaṉ wiped at the eye. The blood kept coming. He looked around for whoever might have hurt the Lord, but no one was there. He ran down the hill to gather herbs that hunters used on wounds. He brought them back and put their juice on the eye.

That did not help either. Tiṇṇaṉ watched the bleeding and searched his mind for something more he could do. The priest watched Tiṇṇaṉ. The two men were in the same shrine, facing the same image, but only one of them could see the hunter's face.

· · ·

Tiṇṇaṉ decided to give his own eye to the bleeding one. He took it out and placed it there. The bleeding stopped. He jumped with relief, as though the Lord had been healed. He had not been thinking of his own loss.

Then blood began to flow from the image's other eye. Tiṇṇaṉ saw that there was still hurt before him. He had one eye left. He moved to give that one too.

Before he could injure his second eye, Śiva reached out and held his hand. Stop, Kaṇṇappar, he called, not once but three times. Tiṇṇaṉ did not have to give the second eye. The Lord gave him a place beside him.

· · ·

The priest had seen why the hunter brought food and water. The acts he had cleared away were the acts of someone who thought the Lord needed feeding and protection. The priest's worship had its care and discipline; the hunter's had a different shape.

For the parent, not read aloud

For the parent: Sēkkiḻār tells an extraordinary saint-life, not an instruction to hurt oneself. The text describes precisely how Tiṇṇaṉ prepared the second act; we leave that method out of the spoken telling. If a child asks, discuss care without self-harm.

At the beginning Tiṇṇaṉ had brought the things he knew: food, flowers and water. At the end, Śiva held his hand. The story lets us remember both movements: a hunter who could not turn away from someone hurt, and a Lord who would not let him give his second eye.

After the story

Have you ever seen someone do something strange, then understood the care behind it later?

The same act can look different when we see the care behind it.

Should someone hurt themselves to show devotion?

No. This is an extraordinary act in Sēkkiḻār's saint-life, and the episode ends with Śiva stopping Tiṇṇaṉ before he hurts his second eye. It is not an instruction for a child or adult to injure themselves. We can care for others while also keeping ourselves safe.

Was the priest wrong to remove the meat and water?

The Periya Purāṇam gives the priest a real duty: he serves the shrine by the worship rules he has learned. The hunter serves out of love in the way he knows. The divine dream asks the priest to watch before judging; the story honours the love he could not see at first.

Why is he called Kaṇṇappar?

In the Periya Purāṇam, Śiva calls him Kaṇṇappar when he stops the second act. The name recalls his eye-gift; Tiṇṇaṉ was the hunter's earlier name.

The Seven Lines Lakṣmaṇa Drew

In one Telugu Rāmāyaṇa, Lakṣmaṇa draws seven lines around Sītā’s hut before leaving to find Rāma. What happens next is not the one-line version many families know.

Short telling · 304 spoken words · timing pending

Sītā saw a deer whose coat flashed with colors no ordinary deer could wear. She asked Rāma to catch it if he could. Lakṣmaṇa looked carefully and warned that it might be a rākṣasa in disguise. Rāma went after it and asked his brother to remain with Sītā at their forest hut.

The deer led Rāma deep among the trees. Each time he came close, it ran; each time it vanished, it appeared again. It was Mārīca, wearing a false shape. When Rāma struck him, Mārīca cried out in a voice that sounded as though someone needed Lakṣmaṇa.

Sītā heard the cry and feared for Rāma. She urged Lakṣmaṇa to go. He knew Rāma was strong, and he feared leaving her alone. Their words grew painful. At last he said he would bring his brother back.

Before he left, Lakṣmaṇa drew seven lines around the hut. He asked Sītā to stay within them. He called on Agni, the fire god, to guard her, bowed to her, and went into the forest.

Rāvaṇa had been waiting. He came to the hut dressed as an ascetic, with the things a holy traveler might carry. Sītā saw a guest and treated him with respect. In doing so, she stepped across the lines. The visitor asked who she was. She told him about herself, Rāma, and the cry that had drawn Lakṣmaṇa away. She did not know who stood before her.

Then the visitor told her his own name: Rāvaṇa. He wanted her to come to his kingdom. Sītā refused. She told him to leave, and warned him of Rāma. Rāvaṇa cast off the disguise and carried her away.

Lakṣmaṇa had drawn his lines with care. Sītā had welcomed someone she believed was a holy guest. The danger came from the one who dressed himself to deceive her.

Full telling · 664 spoken words · timing pending

The deer stood near the forest hut where Rāma, Sītā, and Lakṣmaṇa lived. Its colors shifted as it moved. For a moment its shining side looked like gold; then another color flashed from it. It darted behind the trees and appeared again, close enough to see but never close enough to catch. It seemed to play just beyond the hut, turning its head, leaping, vanishing into the shade and coming back into the light.

Sītā came out while gathering flowers. She watched the strange animal move through the grass. She asked Rāma to bring it to her. If it could not be caught, she wished for its beautiful skin. Lakṣmaṇa did not trust what he saw. An ordinary deer, he said, could not have a body like that. The rākṣasa Mārīca knew how to change his shape, and the brothers had reason to be wary of him. Lakṣmaṇa tried to turn their eyes from the beauty of the deer to the danger hidden inside it.

Rāma listened. Then he took his bow. He would go after the deer, and Lakṣmaṇa would stay to watch over Sītā. The deer ran. Whenever Rāma came near, it sprang away; whenever he lost sight of it, it showed itself again. Mile after mile, it drew him farther from the hut. At last Rāma saw through the trick and struck the animal. As Mārīca fell, his deer-shape vanished. He gave one loud cry, calling for Lakṣmaṇa.

That cry reached the hut. Sītā knew it sounded like trouble for Rāma. She asked Lakṣmaṇa to run to him. Lakṣmaṇa remembered his brother's instruction to stay. Rāma, he said, was not easily overcome; a rākṣasa could be trying to pull them apart. He was afraid of what might happen if he left Sītā alone.

Fear made their talk sharp. Sītā thought Lakṣmaṇa was refusing to help his brother. She accused him of motives he did not have. He was hurt by her words. He called on the forest gods as witnesses to what had been said, then promised to find Rāma and bring him back quickly.

But first Lakṣmaṇa went around the hut and drew seven lines on the ground. He asked Sītā not to cross them. He said that anyone trying to cross would be destroyed at once. He prayed to Agni to keep watch over her, bowed, and followed Rāma into the trees.

Someone else had been waiting for just such a moment. Rāvaṇa approached wearing the clothes and carrying the objects of a wandering ascetic. He leaned forward as he walked and spoke as such a traveler might speak. Sītā did not see the king of Laṅkā. She saw a holy visitor at the door of a forest home.

She greeted him with respect and stepped across the lines Lakṣmaṇa had drawn. The visitor asked about her and the hut. Sītā answered openly: she was Janaka's daughter and Rāma's wife; she and the two brothers lived there during their forest years. Rāma had gone after the shining deer. A terrible cry had followed, and Lakṣmaṇa had gone to find him.

The visitor stopped pretending. He named himself Rāvaṇa, ruler of Laṅkā. He spoke of the riches of his kingdom and asked Sītā to come with him. She refused. She told him that she belonged with Rāma, and warned him to leave before Rāma returned. She would not be persuaded by his promises.

Rāvaṇa grew angry. He dropped the ascetic's appearance and stood before her in his own terrible form. Sītā was frightened, but her answer did not change. He seized her and carried her toward his chariot. She cried out for Rāma and Lakṣmaṇa as he took her into the sky.

In this Telugu telling, the seven lines are there. The source also shows exactly how Sītā crossed them: she was honoring a visitor whom she believed to be an ascetic. Rāvaṇa had chosen that disguise so she would trust him. The choice to deceive and abduct was his.

After the story

When someone seems to need help, what might you look or listen for before you decide what to do?

Kindness can walk beside care.

Did Lakṣmaṇa draw a line in every Rāmāyaṇa?

No. This story follows a Telugu Rāmāyaṇa with seven lines. Vālmīki’s Sanskrit departure scene has no drawn line.

Was Sītā to blame for what Rāvaṇa did?

No. She greeted a visitor whom she believed was an ascetic. Rāvaṇa chose to disguise himself, deceive her, and carry her away.

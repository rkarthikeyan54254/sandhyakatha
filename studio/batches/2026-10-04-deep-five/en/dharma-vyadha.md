# The Teacher in the Meat Market

Source: *Mahābhārata*, Vana Parva, Ganguli §§205–206, 213–214. English review candidate only.

## Short — spoken

«Kauśika» was a learned brāhmaṇa. One day, while he recited beneath a tree, a crane perched above him soiled him. His anger flashed towards the bird, and it fell dead. As soon as he saw what he had done, «Kauśika» was sorry.

Later, he stopped at a house to ask for food. The woman there asked him to wait while she attended to her husband, who had come home hungry. «Kauśika» grew angry again. But the woman knew about the crane. She told him that anger was no sign of wisdom, and directed him to a teacher in «Mithilā»: a man who sold meat.

«Kauśika» travelled to the city and found the seller in the market, busy with buyers. He stood at a distance. The man came to greet him, knew why he had come, and welcomed him home as a guest. «Kauśika» had expected to ask about dharma, but he was troubled by the man's work. The seller did not hide it. He said this was the trade he had inherited. He also spoke of telling the truth, giving what he could, and caring for those who depended on him.

After their long conversation, the man asked «Kauśika» to meet his aged parents. They were seated at home. He bowed to them, and they spoke warmly of the care he gave them. He helped them bathe, washed their feet and gave them food. Here «Kauśika» could see the care the man had spoken about.

Then he spoke of «Kauśika»'s own parents. «Kauśika» had left them to pursue learning. The man urged him to return and care for them. «Kauśika» accepted his words. He had come looking for a lesson from a stranger in a meat market; the stranger had pointed him towards his own aged parents waiting at home.

## Full — spoken

«Kauśika» had studied deeply. He knew the Vedas and spent his days learning and reciting. Once he sat beneath a tree with the words on his lips. A female crane perched above him and soiled his body. «Kauśika» looked up in anger. His angry gaze struck the bird, and she fell from the tree, dead. He regretted what his anger had done.

When «Kauśika» went to a village to ask for food, a woman at one house told him to wait. She was preparing to give him alms, but her husband came home hungry and tired. She gave her husband water, a seat and food. Only then did she remember the visitor standing outside. She brought the food to «Kauśika», ashamed that she had made him wait.

«Kauśika» was angry. He spoke of the respect due to a brāhmaṇa. The woman asked him to let go of that anger. She knew about the crane beneath the tree, though she had not been there. She spoke of truth, forgiveness and control of anger as marks of a wise person. She urged him to seek a man in «Mithilā» who could speak about dharma, the difficult question of how to act well. The man was a seller of meat.

«Kauśika» listened. He had already regretted the crane's death. Now he set out for «Mithilā», passing through forests, villages and towns. When he reached the city, he asked where to find the teacher. The answer led him to a butcher's yard. There the man sold meat to a crowd of buyers. «Kauśika» stood back from the busy place. The seller noticed him, went to him and greeted him respectfully. He even knew why the visitor had come. They walked together to the man's home, where he gave «Kauśika» a seat and water.

At last «Kauśika» could ask the question that troubled him. How could this man speak so well of right conduct while selling meat? The seller did not pretend to have another trade. He said that this work had come down through his family. He described the way he tried to live: speaking truthfully, giving within his means, serving guests and dependants, and not answering wrong with wrong. He sold meat brought by others, he said; he did not slaughter the animals himself. None of this made the work disappear from the story. It was part of what «Kauśika» had to listen to while he weighed the man's words.

Their conversation went on. The seller spoke about actions that can harm and actions that can heal. He spoke of restraining anger, the very thing that «Kauśika» had failed to do beneath the tree and at the woman's door. «Kauśika» listened to a person he might have passed by without asking his name. The man who had been busy with customers was giving his guest time and patient answers.

Then the seller said that «Kauśika» should meet his father and mother. In an inner room, the aged couple sat after their meal. Their son bowed at their feet. They greeted him with affection and spoke of how steadily he cared for them. «Kauśika» asked after their health. The seller explained that he helped them bathe, washed their feet and fed them with his own hands. This was not a lesson he had kept only in words. The people who received his care were sitting before «Kauśika».

The seller now turned the question towards his visitor. «Kauśika» had left his own parents to pursue learning without their permission. The seller said they grieved deeply in his absence and urged him to go home and console them. He asked him to return home that very day, without delay. «Kauśika» did not answer with another angry speech. He thanked the man and accepted his counsel. He had crossed forests and towns to ask a stranger about dharma. The answer pointed towards a duty he had left behind.

## Close

**Question:** What did «Kauśika» see in the teacher's home that he could not learn only by hearing his words?

**Parent aside:** The selected *Mahābhārata* witness presents a meat seller and a caste-bound theory of inherited work; it also presents the householder's marriage through an old duty framework. The spoken edition reports the setting but does not recommend caste assignment or obedience to modern children. §206 says he sells meat killed by others, not that he runs a meatless shop. §§214–215 contain a previous-birth explanation that this candidate leaves to the source note rather than using it to excuse the occupation. The text records Kauśika accepting advice; it does not narrate his later arrival home.

**If they ask — Why did the teacher sell meat?** In this telling he says it is the trade he inherited, and he tells Kauśika that he does not kill the animals he sells (§206). The story still shows the trade plainly. His care for his parents is what he asks Kauśika to see (§213–214).

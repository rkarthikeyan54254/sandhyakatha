Nṛga in the Dry Well

Short telling 308 whitespace words · timing pending

The Yādava boys had played until they were thirsty. They went looking for water, but the well they found was dry. At the bottom was something stranger than water: a lizard so large that it looked almost like a little hill.

They felt sorry for it. They lowered straps, then ropes, and pulled. Nothing moved. So they ran to Kṛṣṇa. He came to the well, reached down with his left hand, and lifted the creature out. At his touch, the lizard became a shining man.

Kṛṣṇa asked who he was. The man bowed. I was King Nṛga, he said. I gave away more cows than I could count. But one gift went wrong.

A cow belonging to a brāhmaṇa had wandered into the king's herd. Nṛga did not know she was there. He gave her to another brāhmaṇa. The first man saw his cow being led away and called out that she was his. The second said the king had given her to him. Both came to Nṛga.

The king offered a hundred thousand fine cows in exchange for that one. He said he had made the gift without knowing the truth. Neither man accepted. Each went away, and the matter stayed unsettled.

After Nṛga died, Yama asked whether he wished to meet the results of his good deeds or this wrong first. Nṛga chose the difficult result first. He became the lizard in the well. Through all that time, he remembered who he had been.

Now the boys watched him standing before Kṛṣṇa. Nṛga bowed again. With Kṛṣṇa's permission, he went away in a heavenly vehicle.

Kṛṣṇa then spoke seriously to those with him about taking property that belonged to a brāhmaṇa, even without knowing it. The cow had not been Nṛga's to give. His great gifts had not made that one cow disappear from the story.

Sāmba, Pradyumna, and other Yādava boys played in a small forest until thirst sent them looking for water.

They found a well. It was dry. Far below sat a lizard so enormous it seemed almost as big as a hill. It could not climb out.

The boys wanted to help. First they tried leather straps. Then they tried woven ropes. They pulled and pulled, but the creature did not rise. At last they went to Kṛṣṇa and told him what they had found.

Kṛṣṇa came with them to the dry well. He looked down, reached in with his left hand, and lifted the great lizard out. The moment his hand touched it, the creature's form changed. A man stood there, clothed and ornamented like a being from heaven. The boys who had struggled with the ropes could now see him standing beside the well.

Kṛṣṇa knew more than he said, but he asked the stranger to tell everyone who he was and how he had come to be there. The man bowed. He was Nṛga, once a king, a son of Ikṣvāku.

Nṛga had given many gifts. He remembered cows with calves, cows whose horns had been gilded and whose hooves were covered in silver. He had given them to brāhmaṇas in need. How many cows? He spoke of numbers as great as the grains of sand or drops of rain.

But among all those gifts there had been one cow that was not his.

She belonged to another brāhmaṇa. She had strayed into the king's herd without Nṛga knowing. When he gave cows away, he gave her to a second man. As that man led her home, her owner saw her. That is my cow, he said. The king gave her to me, replied the man who had received her.

The two men came before Nṛga. Each had a claim to the same animal. One had lost his cow. The other had accepted a gift from the king. Nṛga heard them and was bewildered. What could he do that would put this right for both of them?

He offered a hundred thousand of his best cows in exchange for that single cow. He asked both men to show him mercy. He had given her away without knowing she was another person's, he told them. But the man who had received her would not exchange her. The original owner refused the offered cows too. Both left. Neither man gave way. Nṛga had offered much, but the cow was still claimed by both.

Later Nṛga died. Yama's messengers brought him before Yama, who spoke of the many good results waiting for him from his gifts. Then Yama gave him a choice: would he experience those good results first, or the painful result of the mistaken gift? Nṛga chose the painful one first.

He fell into the body of a lizard. Yet he did not forget his earlier life. He remembered who he had been through that strange life.

And then came a day when some thirsty boys looked down into a dry well. Their ropes could not lift him, but Kṛṣṇa's hand did. Standing once more in a human-shaped, shining form, Nṛga praised Kṛṣṇa. He asked to depart. Kṛṣṇa gave his permission, and Nṛga bowed and entered a heavenly vehicle while the others watched.

Kṛṣṇa spoke next to his companions. His warning was about the property of a brāhmaṇa, even when someone takes it without knowing. Nṛga had been generous beyond counting, but he had also given away a cow that belonged to someone else. Nṛga had offered more cows than most people could count, yet he had watched both men walk away. The one disputed cow remained in his account beside all those gifts.

The well was empty of water. When the boys looked into it, they found a question that had followed Nṛga there for a very long time.

Parent notes, source boundaries and after-story questions

Witness: *Bhāgavata Purāṇa* 10.64.1–44; the events narrated below are in 1–30, with Kṛṣṇa's particular warning to his listeners in 31–44. See `../source-notes/nrga-well.md`. Proposed minimum age: 9. Parent care note: the story includes death, a long punishment as a lizard, and an unresolved dispute over a cow given by mistake. The source's closing warning concerns brāhmaṇa property in particular; the spoken ending must not turn its caste-specific framing into a falsely universal maxim.

Parent-facing close and questions (draft)

  • Open question: If you had been standing beside Nṛga and the two men, what would you have wanted each person to hear?
  • Seed, only if needed: A generous act cannot give away what was not ours to give.
  • If they ask, “Why did he become a lizard for a mistake?”: The *Bhāgavata* says the cow entered his herd without his knowledge, yet the dispute remained unresolved. Yama offered Nṛga a choice of which result to face first, and he chose the painful one. The text does not ask us to call the punishment proportionate; we can notice how severe it is.
  • If they ask, “Why didn't they accept all those other cows?”: The passage tells us both refused, but does not give either man's private reasoning. One had lost his own cow; the other had received that very cow as a gift. We should not invent a settlement the source does not show.
  • If they ask, “Was Kṛṣṇa warning everyone about any property?”: His speech here specifically warns his royal listeners about taking a brāhmaṇa's property, even unknowingly. We can discuss care with others' belongings without erasing that particular setting of the source.

सूखे कुएँ में राजा नृग — हिन्दी समीक्षा मसौदा

यदुवंश के कुछ लड़के खेलते-खेलते प्यासे हो गए। पानी की खोज में उन्हें एक सूखा कुआँ मिला। भीतर पानी नहीं था; पहाड़ी जितनी बड़ी छिपकली फँसी थी। लड़कों को उस पर दया आई। उन्होंने चमड़े की पट्टियों और रस्सियों से उसे निकालना चाहा, पर वह हिली तक नहीं। तब वे दौड़कर कृष्ण को बुला लाए।

कृष्ण ने बायाँ हाथ बढ़ाया और उसे कुएँ से उठा लिया। उनके स्पर्श से उसका रूप बदल गया। अब सामने चमकते वस्त्रों और आभूषणों वाला एक दिव्य पुरुष खड़ा था। कृष्ण ने पूछा, “तुम कौन हो? कुएँ में कैसे पहुँचे?”

उसने अपना नाम बताया—राजा नृग। वह बहुत-सी गायें दान कर चुका था। एक दिन किसी ब्राह्मण की गाय भटककर उसके झुंड में आ मिली। राजा को पता नहीं चला और उसने वही गाय दूसरे ब्राह्मण को दान दे दी। पहला मालिक उसे ले जाते देखकर बोला, “यह मेरी गाय है!” दूसरे ने कहा, “राजा ने मुझे दी है!” दोनों राजा के पास पहुँचे।

नृग ने अपनी भूल मानी। उसने एक गाय के बदले एक लाख अच्छी गायें देने की विनती की। पर जिसे गाय दान में मिली थी, वह अपनी गाय छोड़ना नहीं चाहता था। पहला मालिक भी बदले की गायें लेकर नहीं गया। दोनों चले गए; विवाद वैसा ही रह गया।

बाद में मृत्यु के देवता यम ने नृग से पूछा कि वह अपने कर्मों के किस फल को पहले भोगना चाहता है। राजा ने कठिन फल पहले चुना और छिपकली बन गया। उसे अपना पिछला जीवन याद रहा। फिर उस सूखे कुएँ में कृष्ण से उसकी भेंट हुई। राजा ने उन्हें प्रणाम किया, विदा माँगी और स्वर्ग की ओर जाने वाले विमान पर बैठ गया।

जिस गाय को राजा लौटाना चाहता था, उसके बारे में दोनों मालिकों की बात हमें सुनाई देती है। कथा में कोई आसान समझौता नहीं हुआ। फिर भी कुएँ में फँसे जीव को देखकर लड़के रुके, मदद करने की कोशिश की और कृष्ण को बुलाया।

पूछें: दोनों ब्राह्मण अपनी-अपनी बात पर क्यों अड़े होंगे? राजा और क्या कर सकता था—या क्या सचमुच उसके पास कोई साफ़ रास्ता नहीं था?

यदि बच्चा चुप रहे: किसी विवाद में दोनों पक्षों की बात सुनना ज़रूरी है।

खेलते हुए यदुवंश के लड़कों को प्यास लगी। वे जंगल में पानी ढूँढ़ने लगे। एक कुआँ दिखा, मगर वह सूखा था। उसके तल में कोई विशाल जीव था—पहाड़ी जितनी बड़ी छिपकली। लड़के पहले चौंके, फिर देखा कि वह बाहर नहीं आ पा रही है। उन्हें दया आई। चमड़े की पट्टियाँ डालीं, रस्सियाँ लगाईं, सबने मिलकर खींचा। इतनी कोशिश के बाद भी उसे ऊपर न उठा सके। वे कृष्ण के पास गए और कुएँ की बात बताई।

कृष्ण उनके साथ आए। उन्होंने कुएँ में झाँका और बायाँ हाथ बढ़ाकर छिपकली को बाहर निकाल लिया। जैसे ही उनका हाथ लगा, वह रूप बदलकर दिव्य पुरुष हो गई। सुनहरे रंग की देह पर सुंदर वस्त्र और आभूषण थे। लड़के जिस जीव को रस्सियों से न उठा सके थे, वही अब उनके सामने अपने पैरों पर खड़ा था। कृष्ण ने पूछा कि वह कौन है और इस दशा में कैसे पहुँचा। सामने खड़े पुरुष ने प्रणाम किया और अपनी बात शुरू की।

“मैं नृग हूँ, इक्ष्वाकु का पुत्र,” उसने कहा। “जब मैं राजा था, बहुत-सी गायें दान करता था।” उसने उन गायों का वर्णन किया—बछड़ों सहित, दूध देने वाली, अच्छे ढंग से पाली गई गायें। जिन्हें गायें मिलतीं, उनकी ज़रूरतों का भी राजा ध्यान रखता था। अपने दान की गिनती वह रेत के कणों और बारिश की बूँदों से करता था।

फिर उसने उस एक गाय की बात कही जिससे उलझन शुरू हुई। वह किसी दूसरे ब्राह्मण की थी। भटककर नृग के बड़े झुंड में आ मिली। राजा को इसका पता नहीं चला। उसने उसी गाय को एक ब्राह्मण के हाथ दान में दे दिया। जब पहला मालिक उसे ले जाते देख रहा था, उसने रोककर कहा, “यह मेरी है।” दूसरा बोला, “मुझे राजा ने दी है।” एक गाय थी; उस पर दोनों अपना अधिकार बता रहे थे।

दोनों नृग के पास आए। एक ने कहा कि राजा की दी हुई गाय अब उसकी है। दूसरे ने कहा कि राजा ने उसकी गाय किसी और को कैसे दे दी। राजा उलझ गया। उसे समझ आ गया कि बिना जाने उससे भूल हुई है। उसने दोनों से विनती की कि इस एक गाय के बदले वे एक लाख उत्तम गायें ले लें और उसे क्षमा करें। वह गाय वापस लेकर मामला सुलझाना चाहता था।

पर वह जिसे दान में मिली थी, गाय बदलना नहीं चाहता था। पहला मालिक भी बदले में दी जा रही हजारों गायें लेकर नहीं माना। दोनों अपनी-अपनी बात कहते हुए चले गए। न गाय लौटने का समझौता हुआ, न दोनों का विवाद मिटा। नृग ने यह हिस्सा कृष्ण से छिपाया नहीं। उसने साफ़ कहा कि उसने अनजाने में दूसरे की गाय दान दी थी और उसे वापस पाने की उसकी कोशिश सफल नहीं हुई।

कुछ समय बाद राजा का जीवन समाप्त हुआ। मृत्यु के देवता यम ने उससे कहा कि उसके दान का अच्छा फल बहुत है, पर एक कठिन फल भी उसके सामने है। पहले किसे भोगेगा? नृग ने कठिन फल पहले चुना। उसी समय वह छिपकली बनकर गिरा। इस रूप में भी उसे अपनी पिछली बातों की याद बनी रही। बरसों बाद वही छिपकली लड़कों को सूखे कुएँ में मिली।

अब कृष्ण के सामने खड़े नृग ने उन्हें प्रणाम किया। उसे जाने की अनुमति मिली। सबकी आँखों के सामने वह एक आकाश में चलने वाले विमान पर बैठकर चला गया। उसके जाने के बाद कृष्ण ने अपने साथियों को सावधान किया कि ब्राह्मण की संपत्ति को, अनजाने में भी, लेना कितनी गंभीर बात है। उनकी चेतावनी इसी कथा के अंत में आती है।

कुएँ की बात तो पूरी हुई: लड़कों ने एक फँसे जीव को देखा, कृष्ण ने उसे निकाला, और उसने अपना पुराना नाम बताया। गाय का झगड़ा कहानी में किसी के मान जाने से खत्म नहीं होता। नृग ने बहुत दान किया था, फिर भी इस एक गाय के बारे में वह दोनों दावों को एक साथ सही नहीं कर सका। उसकी बात सुनकर हम पूछ सकते हैं कि भूल पहचानना, भरपाई करना और न्याय करना—इनमें से हर काम कितना अलग है।

पूछें: अगर एक ही गाय पर दो लोगों का दावा हो और दोनों बदले में दूसरी गाय न लें, तो राजा किसकी बात पहले सुने?

यदि बच्चा चुप रहे: राजा ने अपनी भूल मानी, लेकिन दोनों लोगों की सहमति उसे नहीं मिली।

Parent notes

मूल अध्याय में ब्राह्मण की संपत्ति के बारे में विशेष चेतावनी, नरक और कई पीढ़ियों तक विनाश के कथन हैं; १०.६४.१४–१५ में स्त्रियों को भी दान की वस्तुओं में गिनाया गया है। इन्हें आज के बच्चों के लिए सामान्य नैतिक नियम बनाकर न सुनाएँ। दोनों ब्राह्मणों ने बदले की गायें अस्वीकार कीं; कथा में उन्हें किसी ने शाप देते नहीं दिखाया। दंड उचित था या नहीं, यह प्रश्न बच्चे से दबाने की ज़रूरत नहीं। प्रारंभिक आयु-सुझाव ९+ है, अंतिम आयु-निर्णय संपादकीय समीक्षा के बाद हो।

यदि बच्चा पूछे, “क्या दोनों ब्राह्मणों ने शाप दिया?” नहीं। इस चुने हुए अध्याय के श्लोक १६–२४ में विवाद, असफल विनती और फिर यम का प्रश्न आता है। बोला गया शाप नहीं मिलता।

Indra Returns to Ask Again

Short telling 322 whitespace words · timing pending

Prajāpati spoke of a Self that did not grow old or die. Indra, from the gods, and Virocana, from the asuras, both came to learn what he meant. They lived with him as students for thirty-two years.

At last Prajāpati spoke of the person seen in the eye. His students asked about the person seen in water or a mirror. He told them to look into a pan of water. They saw themselves, down to their hair and nails. Then he asked them to dress well and look again. The reflections wore the same fine clothes. Both students went away satisfied.

Virocana returned to the asuras and taught that the body was the Self. Prajāpati knew that neither student had understood his teaching. Before reaching the gods, Indra stopped. The reflection changed whenever the body changed; it would disappear when the body died. How could that be the Self Prajāpati had called deathless? Indra went back to ask.

After another thirty-two years, Prajāpati pointed to the person who moves in dreams. Indra left, but turned back again. A dreamer might be chased, hurt or frightened. That answer did not yet give him the fearless Self he sought.

Indra studied for thirty-two more years. Now Prajāpati spoke of dreamless sleep, when a person rests without even a dream. Indra returned once more: in such sleep, a person seems to know neither himself nor anything around him. He could not stop his question there.

Five more years passed. Then Prajāpati gave his final teaching. The body can die, he said, but it is the dwelling place of the deathless, bodiless Self. He spoke of wind and cloud taking form in the light, and of the Self appearing in its own form. Indra had stayed a student for one hundred and one years. Each time an answer seemed complete, he had looked at it carefully and returned to ask what he had not yet understood.

Prajāpati had said that there is a Self free from old age, death and sorrow. The gods heard his words. So did the asuras. Each wanted to understand the Self he had described. Indra came from the gods, and Virocana came from the asuras. They carried firewood, as students did when approaching a teacher, and lived with Prajāpati for thirty-two years.

When the years had passed, he asked why they had come. They repeated his teaching back to him: this was the Self they wanted to know. Prajāpati spoke of the person seen in the eye. His students asked whether the person visible in water or in a mirror was the same one. He told them that the same was seen in all of these, and asked them to look into a pan of water.

They looked. What could they see? Their own shapes, they said, even the hair and nails. Prajāpati told them to put on fine clothes and ornaments and look again. They did. The figures in the water wore fine clothes and ornaments too. That answer satisfied both students, and they left together.

But Prajāpati watched them go. Neither had understood the Self he meant. Virocana reached the asuras and taught that the body itself should be honored and served. Indra did not reach the gods. On the way, he noticed a difficulty. The water showed whatever happened to the body. If the body changed, the reflection changed. When the body died, the reflection would be gone. Was that the deathless Self they had come to learn about?

Carrying firewood again, Indra returned. He told Prajāpati what troubled him. His teacher asked him to remain another thirty-two years. Then he offered a new answer: the one who moves about in dreams is the Self.

Indra set out. Once more, he stopped before reaching the gods. A person in a dream could be chased. He might seem to be hurt, afraid or in tears. The dream did not answer his question about a fearless Self. Again he brought firewood to Prajāpati, and again he said why he had returned. The teacher told him to stay for thirty-two more years.

After that long study, Prajāpati spoke of a person in deep, dreamless sleep. Here was complete rest, with no frightening dream at all. Indra went away satisfied for a while. Yet another question came before he reached the gods. In such sleep, the sleeper does not know, “I am here.” Nor does he know the beings around him. That sounded to Indra almost like disappearance. How could he stop there?

He came back with firewood a third time. Prajāpati heard his concern and asked him to stay five more years. By then Indra had studied with him for one hundred and one years. The teacher had not given him one easy sentence to carry home. At each stage, Indra had found what the answer left unexplained.

Now Prajāpati spoke of the mortal body as the dwelling place of a deathless Self. While someone lives in a body, pleasure and pain reach that life. The Self he was describing was not merely the figure in a pan of water, the frightened figure in a dream, or the blankness Indra had worried about in sleep. Prajāpati spoke of wind and cloud, which can appear in their own forms when they rise into the light. He said the Self too appears in its own form.

Virocana had taken the first reflection home as a finished answer. Indra had looked again, then asked again. The water, the dream and the deep sleep had each shown him something, but none had ended his search. His teacher's final words came after all those returns. They were not the first answer heard beside the pan of water. The long years matter in this telling because Indra did not call a question settled just because he had already spent years trying to answer it.

गुरु ने दूसरी बार भी उन्हें सुना। उन्होंने फिर बत्तीस वर्ष रहने को कहा। इस बार प्रजापति ने गहरी, सपनों से खाली नींद का उदाहरण दिया। वहाँ न पीछा है, न डरावना सपना। इन्द्र संतुष्ट होकर चले, पर देवताओं तक पहुँचने से पहले फिर रुक गए। ऐसी नींद में सोने वाले को “मैं हूँ” का भी भान नहीं रहता; आसपास के प्राणियों का भी नहीं। वह अनुभव तो लगभग कुछ न जानने जैसा था। इससे भी उनका प्रश्न समाप्त नहीं हुआ।

तीसरी बार इन्द्र समिधा लेकर गुरु के पास पहुँचे। प्रजापति ने कहा कि पाँच वर्ष और रहो। इन पाँच वर्षों को जोड़कर उनकी पढ़ाई एक सौ एक वर्ष की हुई। पहले पानी, फिर स्वप्न, फिर गहरी नींद—हर उत्तर के बाद उन्होंने देखा कि कौन-सी बात अभी बाकी है। गुरु ने भी हर बार आगे पढ़ाया।

अब प्रजापति ने नश्वर देह और अमर, देह से परे आत्मा में अंतर बताया। देह में रहते हुए सुख और दुख आते हैं। मगर उनके अंतिम कथन की आत्मा केवल पानी में दिखाई देता चेहरा नहीं थी। न वह केवल सपने में डरने वाला था, न ऐसी नींद की खाली-सी स्थिति जिससे इन्द्र उलझे थे। उन्होंने हवा और बादल का उदाहरण दिया: प्रकाश में वे अपने रूप में प्रकट होते हैं। आत्मा के अपने रूप में प्रकट होने की बात भी उन्होंने कही।

इन्द्र को यह उत्तर पहले दिन नहीं मिला था। उन्होंने पहले पानी में अपना चेहरा देखा था और संतुष्ट होकर चले भी गए थे। फिर लौटे, फिर पढ़े, और दो बार और लौटे। विरोचन उसी शुरुआती प्रतिबिंब को पर्याप्त मानकर घर गए थे। दोनों की राह एक ही जल-पात्र से शुरू हुई थी, पर आगे अलग हुई। इन्द्र ने हर बार उस चीज़ पर ध्यान दिया जो पुराने उत्तर से नहीं सुलझ रही थी।

அவர்கள் சென்றதும் பிரஜாபதி சொன்னார்: இருவரும் புரிந்துகொள்ளாமலே போய்விட்டார்கள். அந்தச் சொல் அவர்களுக்குக் கேட்கவில்லை. விரோசனன் அசுரர்களிடம் சென்று, உடலையே போற்றி அதற்குச் சேவை செய்வதே வழி என்று கற்பித்தார்.

அவர் திரும்பிப் போய் பிரஜாபதி என்னும் ஆசிரியரிடம் தன் சந்தேகத்தைச் சொன்னார். மேலும் முப்பத்திரண்டு ஆண்டுகள் தங்கினார். இம்முறை ஆசிரியர் கனவில் நகரும் ஒருவனைப் பற்றிச் சொன்னார். தூக்கத்தில் உடல் படுத்திருக்கும்போது கனவில் நாம் வேறு இடங்களுக்குச் செல்கிறோம்; அப்போது தோன்றுபவனே ஆத்மா என்றார்.

இந்திரன் புறப்பட்டார். ஆனால் மறுபடியும் பாதியில் நின்றார். கனவில் உடலுக்குப் பட்ட குறைபாடுகள் தெரியாமல் இருக்கலாம். இருந்தாலும் கனவில் ஒருவன் பயப்படுகிறான், ஓடுகிறான், துன்பப்படுகிறான். துன்பம் இல்லாத ஆத்மாவைத் தேடியவர் இதையே முடிவாக ஏற்க முடியவில்லை. அவர் மீண்டும் ஆசிரியரிடம் வந்தார்.

இன்னும் முப்பத்திரண்டு ஆண்டுகள் படித்தபின், ஆழ்ந்த உறக்கத்தில் அமைதியாக இருக்கும் நிலையைப் பற்றி பிரஜாபதி சொன்னார். இந்திரன் சென்றார்; மீண்டும் கேள்வி எழுந்தது. ஆழ்ந்த தூக்கத்தில் நான் என்பதும் சுற்றியுள்ளவர்களும் தெரியாதபோது, அங்கே அறிதல் எங்கே இருக்கிறது? அவர் திரும்பிச் சென்றார். மேலும் ஐந்து ஆண்டுகள் காத்திருந்தார்.

இப்போது பிரஜாபதி அழியும் உடலையும், அதனால் கட்டுப்படாத ஆத்மாவையும் வேறுபடுத்திக் கூறினார். உடல் மாறுவது உண்மை. வலியும் இன்பமும் அதன் வாழ்வில் வரும். ஆனால் அவர் கற்பித்த ஆத்மாவை நீரில் தெரியும் நிழலுக்கோ, கனவில் வரும் காட்சிக்கோ, ஒன்றும் உணராததுபோல் தோன்றும் உறக்கத்துக்கோ மட்டும் ஒப்பிட முடியாது. உடலைத் தாண்டிய அதன் இயல்பைப் பற்றி அவர் விளக்கினார்.

இந்திரன் கற்ற ஆண்டுகளைச் சேர்த்தால் நூற்றொன்று. முதல் நாளில் தண்ணீரைக் குனிந்து பார்த்த இருவருக்கும் தத்தம் உருவம் தெரிந்தது; இருவரும் ஒரே பதிலோடு புறப்பட்டார்கள். ஒருவருக்கு அது முடிவாயிற்று. மற்றொருவர் ஒவ்வொரு பதிலையும் தன் கேள்வியோடு பார்த்தார். இது போதுமா? என்று திரும்பியதால், இந்திரன் இன்னும் ஆழமாகக் கற்க முடிந்தது.

The Teacher in the Meat Market

Short telling 300 whitespace words · timing pending

Kauśika was a learned brāhmaṇa. One day, while he recited beneath a tree, a crane perched above him soiled him. His anger flashed towards the bird, and it fell dead. As soon as he saw what he had done, Kauśika was sorry.

Later, he stopped at a house to ask for food. The woman there asked him to wait while she attended to her husband, who had come home hungry. Kauśika grew angry again. But the woman knew about the crane. She told him that anger was no sign of wisdom, and directed him to a teacher in Mithilā: a man who sold meat.

Kauśika travelled to the city and found the seller in the market, busy with buyers. He stood at a distance. The man came to greet him, knew why he had come, and welcomed him home as a guest. Kauśika had expected to ask about dharma, but he was troubled by the man's work. The seller did not hide it. He said this was the trade he had inherited. He also spoke of telling the truth, giving what he could, and caring for those who depended on him.

After their long conversation, the man asked Kauśika to meet his aged parents. They were seated at home. He bowed to them, and they spoke warmly of the care he gave them. He helped them bathe, washed their feet and gave them food. Here Kauśika could see the care the man had spoken about.

Then he spoke of Kauśika's own parents. Kauśika had left them to pursue learning. The man urged him to return and care for them. Kauśika accepted his words. He had come looking for a lesson from a stranger in a meat market; the stranger had pointed him towards his own aged parents waiting at home.

Kauśika had studied deeply. He knew the Vedas and spent his days learning and reciting. Once he sat beneath a tree with the words on his lips. A female crane perched above him and soiled his body. Kauśika looked up in anger. His angry gaze struck the bird, and she fell from the tree, dead. He regretted what his anger had done.

When Kauśika went to a village to ask for food, a woman at one house told him to wait. She was preparing to give him alms, but her husband came home hungry and tired. She gave her husband water, a seat and food. Only then did she remember the visitor standing outside. She brought the food to Kauśika, ashamed that she had made him wait.

Kauśika was angry. He spoke of the respect due to a brāhmaṇa. The woman asked him to let go of that anger. She knew about the crane beneath the tree, though she had not been there. She spoke of truth, forgiveness and control of anger as marks of a wise person. She urged him to seek a man in Mithilā who could speak about dharma, the difficult question of how to act well. The man was a seller of meat.

Kauśika listened. He had already regretted the crane's death. Now he set out for Mithilā, passing through forests, villages and towns. When he reached the city, he asked where to find the teacher. The answer led him to a butcher's yard. There the man sold meat to a crowd of buyers. Kauśika stood back from the busy place. The seller noticed him, went to him and greeted him respectfully. He even knew why the visitor had come. They walked together to the man's home, where he gave Kauśika a seat and water.

At last Kauśika could ask the question that troubled him. How could this man speak so well of right conduct while selling meat? The seller did not pretend to have another trade. He said that this work had come down through his family. He described the way he tried to live: speaking truthfully, giving within his means, serving guests and dependants, and not answering wrong with wrong. He sold meat brought by others, he said; he did not slaughter the animals himself. None of this made the work disappear from the story. It was part of what Kauśika had to listen to while he weighed the man's words.

Their conversation went on. The seller spoke about actions that can harm and actions that can heal. He spoke of restraining anger, the very thing that Kauśika had failed to do beneath the tree and at the woman's door. Kauśika listened to a person he might have passed by without asking his name. The man who had been busy with customers was giving his guest time and patient answers.

Then the seller said that Kauśika should meet his father and mother. In an inner room, the aged couple sat after their meal. Their son bowed at their feet. They greeted him with affection and spoke of how steadily he cared for them. Kauśika asked after their health. The seller explained that he helped them bathe, washed their feet and fed them with his own hands. This was not a lesson he had kept only in words. The people who received his care were sitting before Kauśika.

The seller now turned the question towards his visitor. Kauśika had left his own parents to pursue learning without their permission. The seller said they grieved deeply in his absence and urged him to go home and console them. He asked him to return home that very day, without delay. Kauśika did not answer with another angry speech. He thanked the man and accepted his counsel. He had crossed forests and towns to ask a stranger about dharma. The answer pointed towards a duty he had left behind.

Parent aside: The selected *Mahābhārata* witness presents a meat seller and a caste-bound theory of inherited work; it also presents the householder's marriage through an old duty framework. The spoken edition reports the setting but does not recommend caste assignment or obedience to modern children. §206 says he sells meat killed by others, not that he runs a meatless shop. §§214–215 contain a previous-birth explanation that this candidate leaves to the source note rather than using it to excuse the occupation. The text records Kauśika accepting advice; it does not narrate his later arrival home.

If they ask — Why did the teacher sell meat? In this telling he says it is the trade he inherited, and he tells Kauśika that he does not kill the animals he sells (§206). The story still shows the trade plainly. His care for his parents is what he asks Kauśika to see (§213–214).

कौशिक पेड़ के नीचे वेद पढ़ रहे थे। ऊपर बैठी सारस चिड़िया ने उन्हें गंदा कर दिया। उन्हें इतना क्रोध आया कि उनके देखते-देखते चिड़िया मरकर गिर पड़ी। कौशिक को तुरंत दुख हुआ। उन्होंने माना कि क्रोध में उन्होंने बुरा काम किया है।

भिक्षा माँगने वे एक घर पहुँचे। भीतर से स्त्री ने कुछ देर रुकने को कहा। उसी समय उसका भूखा पति घर आया। उसने पहले उसे पानी और भोजन दिया, फिर भिक्षा लेकर बाहर आई। देर होने पर कौशिक नाराज़ हुए। स्त्री ने क्षमा माँगी, पर यह भी कहा, “मैं वह सारस नहीं हूँ। अपने क्रोध को पहचानिए।” उसने उन्हें मिथिला के एक मांस बेचने वाले से धर्म की बात पूछने को कहा।

कौशिक मिथिला गए। दुकान पर हिरन और भैंसे का मांस बिक रहा था; ग्राहकों की भीड़ थी। वे दूर खड़े रहे। दुकानदार स्वयं उनके पास आया, उनका स्वागत किया और घर चलने को कहा। कौशिक को उसका काम देखकर संकोच हुआ। उन्होंने पूछा कि इतने कठोर व्यापार के साथ वह धर्म की बात कैसे जानता है।

उस व्यक्ति ने कहा कि यह उसके परिवार का पुराना काम है। वह सच बोलने और अपनी सामर्थ्य भर देने की कोशिश करता है। फिर वह कौशिक को अपने घर के भीतर ले गया। वहाँ उसके वृद्ध माता-पिता बैठे थे। उसने उनके चरण छुए। वे उसके देखभाल से प्रसन्न थे। वह उन्हें भोजन देता, नहलाने में सहायता करता और उनकी ज़रूरतों पर ध्यान रखता था।

फिर उसने कौशिक से कहा कि उनके अपने माता-पिता उन्हें याद कर रहे हैं। वे घर से दूर रहकर दुखी हैं; उन्हें लौटना चाहिए। कौशिक ने उसकी बात स्वीकार की। रास्ता उन्हें उस घर की स्त्री ने बताया था। सीख उन्हें मांस की दुकान पर मिली। और जिस क्रोध ने सारस की जान ली थी, उसे वे अब अनदेखा नहीं कर सकते थे।

நேரம் செல்லச் செல்ல அவருக்குக் கோபம் ஏறியது. பெண் வந்ததும் அவர் கடுமையாகக் கேட்டார். அவள் பணிவுடன் பதிலளித்தாள்; அதே சமயம் அவரது கோபத்திற்குப் பயந்து தன் பேச்சை மாற்றவில்லை. என்னை அந்தக் கொக்காக நினைக்க வேண்டாம், என்றாள். அந்தக் கொக்குக்கு என்ன ஆனது என்று இவளுக்கு எப்படித் தெரியும் என கௌசிகர் வியந்தார்.

அவள் அவரிடம் அறம் பற்றிப் பேசினாள். கோபத்தைக் கட்டுப்படுத்துவது, பிறருக்கு ஊறு செய்யாமல் இருப்பது, தன் பொறுப்பை உணர்வது—இவற்றை அவர் சிந்திக்கச் சொன்னாள். தன்னிடம் எல்லாவற்றிற்கும் விடை இருப்பதாகக் கூறவில்லை. மேலும் தெரிந்துகொள்ள மிதிலைக்குச் சென்று அங்குள்ள ஒரு வேடனைச் சந்திக்கும்படி வழி காட்டினாள்.

கௌசிகர் பயணித்து மிதிலையை அடைந்தார். சந்தையில் அவர் தேடிய வேடன் இறைச்சிக் கடையில் நின்றான். மான் இறைச்சியும் எருமை இறைச்சியும் அங்கே விற்கப்பட்டன. இவ்வளவு தூரம் வந்தபின் இப்படிப்பட்ட தொழில் செய்பவரிடம் கேட்க வேண்டுமா என்று கௌசிகர் தயங்கினார். அவர் கடைக்குச் சற்றுத் தள்ளியே நின்றார்.

வேடன் அவரைக் கண்டான். மரியாதையுடன் வரவேற்றான். அவர் ஏன் வந்திருக்கிறார் என்பதையும் அறிந்திருந்தான். கடையின் வேலையை முடித்துவிட்டு பேசுவதற்கு ஏற்ற இடத்துக்கு அழைத்துச் சென்றான். கௌசிகர் நேராகக் கேட்டார்: இப்படி இறைச்சி விற்கும் வேலையில் இருக்கும்போது அறத்தைப் பற்றி எப்படிப் பேசுகிறாய்?

வேடன் தன் வாழ்க்கையை மறைக்கவில்லை. இந்தத் தொழில் தன் குடும்பத்தில் வந்தது என்றான். தான் விற்கும் இறைச்சியைத் தானே கொன்று பெறுவதில்லை என்றும் சொன்னான். உண்மை பேசுவதையும், பிறரைக் காயப்படுத்தாமல் இருப்பதையும், பெற்றோரைக் கவனிப்பதையும் தனது கடமைகளாகக் கூறினான். கௌசிகர் கேட்ட கேள்விகளுக்கு அவன் தன் வாழ்க்கையில் நடந்தவற்றைக் கொண்டே பதிலளித்தான்.

அவன் கௌசிகர் என்ற விருந்தினரைத் தன் வீட்டிற்கு அழைத்துச் சென்றான். அங்கே வயதான தாய் தந்தை இருந்தார்கள். அவர்களைப் பார்த்துக்கொள்ளும் விதத்தைத் தன் விருந்தினருக்குக் காட்டினான். அவர்கள் அவனிடம் கொண்ட அன்பையும் கௌசிகர் கண்டார். கடையில் இருந்த மனிதனையும் வீட்டில் பெற்றோரிடம் இருந்த மகனையும் தனித்தனியாகப் பார்க்க முடியவில்லை.

அப்போது வேடன் கௌசிகர் தன் பெற்றோரை நினைக்கும்படி நினைவூட்டினான். அவர் வேதம் ஓதுவதற்காக அவர்களுடைய அனுமதி பெறாமல் வீட்டைவிட்டு வந்திருந்தார். வயதான பெற்றோர் அவரைப் பிரிந்து வருந்துவதாக வேடன் சொன்னான். அவர்களிடம் திரும்பிச் செல்லுங்கள், என்றான். கௌசிகர் அவன் சொன்னதை ஏற்று, பெற்றோரைக் கவனிப்பதாக உறுதியளித்தார். தன்னுடைய வீட்டிலிருந்து தொடங்கும் பொறுப்பை கௌசிகர் இனி தவிர்க்க முடியாது என்பதே அவன் சொன்னது.

கதை தொடங்கிய இடத்தில் ஒரு கொக்கு இறந்தது. பின்னர் ஒரு வீட்டுப் பெண் கௌசிகர் காட்டிய கோபத்தை நிறுத்திப் பேசினாள். இப்போது, அவர் எதிர்பாராத கடையில் சந்தித்த வேடன், தன் பெற்றோரைக் கவனித்துக்கொண்டே அவருக்குப் பதில் சொன்னான். கௌசிகர் தேடிய அறம், அவர் முன்கூட்டியே தேர்ந்தெடுத்த ஒரே தோற்றத்தில் நின்றிருக்கவில்லை.

The Mountain That Offered Hanumān Rest

Short telling 300 whitespace words · timing pending

Hanumān was crossing the sea to look for Sītā, on Rāma's urgent errand. The far shore was still ahead. Below him, the ocean watched him fly on Rāma's work and wished to help. Deep under the water stood a mountain called Maināka, whose peaks shone like gold. The ocean asked the mountain to rise and offer the traveller a place to rest.

The sea opened. Up came Maināka, carrying trees and creepers on his slopes. His bright peaks rose directly into Hanumān's path. From the sky, this sudden mountain looked like something blocking the way. Hanumān struck it with his chest. But Maināka was not trying to stop him. The mountain greeted him and offered him a resting place before the long flight continued.

Maināka explained why he wanted to welcome this particular guest. Long ago, mountains had wings. When Indra began cutting those wings away, Vāyu, the wind and Hanumān's father, carried Maināka to safety. Maināka had not forgotten that help. Now Vāyu's son was passing overhead, doing a difficult task for Rāma. The mountain could offer him its slopes, its welcome, and a little rest.

Hanumān heard him. He honoured the mountain's kindness, but he could not stay. He had set out to find Sītā, and he had promised to finish the crossing without delay. He touched Maināka with his hand and rose into the sky again. Mountain and ocean looked after him with respect and blessings.

Hanumān flew on toward the far shore, following the way of his father, the wind. Maināka remained in the sea, pleased that he had helped as he could. Even Indra praised the mountain for offering shelter to Rāma's messenger. The offered rest and the urgent journey had met for one moment above the water. Then each continued in its own way.

Hanumān had left the shore behind. He was flying across the sea to find Sītā, who had been taken to Laṅkā. His body moved high above the waves, while his shadow travelled over the water below. The wind did not hinder him. The sun did not burn him. Clouds of different colours passed around him. Yet the crossing was long, and the far shore was still out of sight.

The ocean saw Hanumān on his way. It remembered the house of Rāma, for King Sagara of that line had once enlarged the sea. Now Rāma's messenger was crossing it. The ocean wanted to help him finish this difficult journey. It chose to offer a place to rest along the way.

Beneath the water stood Maināka, a mountain with gold-coloured peaks. The ocean asked him to rise. It told him that the traveller above was Hanumān, working for Rāma, and that a resting place would help him cross the remaining distance. Maināka listened. Then his mountain body rose through the sea. Water fell from his slopes. Trees and creepers came up with him, and his shining peaks reached towards the sky. The peaks were so bright that the sky above the water seemed touched by gold. The sea had hidden this mountain; now it stood before the traveller in full view.

To Hanumān, the mountain appeared without warning in the path of his flight. He took it for an obstacle and struck it with his chest. Maināka did not answer the blow with anger. He recognized the traveller's power and took human form to speak to him with respect. He offered his peaks as a place to pause. There was room to sit and rest before continuing over the water. A mountain that had first looked like a barrier was speaking as a host.

Maināka had a reason to care for this guest. He told Hanumān about a much older time, when mountains could fly with wings. They moved where they wished. People were frightened of the mountains that could fall from the sky, and Indra began cutting away their wings. When Maināka was in danger, Vāyu, the wind, swept him to safety and set him under the sea. There he stayed, protected beneath the water. Vāyu was Hanumān's father. The mountain remembered that rescue and wished to welcome the son of the one who had helped him. The old kindness had not been forgotten under the waves.

There was another reason. The ocean had asked Maināka to serve Rāma's messenger. Maināka offered Hanumān rest, hospitality and fruit growing on his slopes. He wanted him to ease the strain of flight and accept the welcome of both mountain and sea. The offer was real. Hanumān could have stopped there for a while. The mountain asked him to regard the welcome with affection, not as an interruption of their shared wish for Rāma's work to succeed.

Hanumān answered with courtesy. He was glad of Maināka's kindness, and said that his words had already given him welcome. But he had a task that could not wait: Sītā had to be found. He had also told the other vānaras that he would cross without stopping. The meeting was brief, yet he did not pass as though the mountain were nothing. He touched Maināka with his hand, giving the welcome the respect he could give, and sprang back into the open sky.

From below, mountain and ocean watched him continue. Hanumān went forward on the path of his father, the wind. Maināka had not given him a long rest, but the mountain had offered it freely. Indra saw what had happened and praised Maināka for helping Hanumān in a dangerous undertaking. The mountain was glad of that praise.

Above the sea, Hanumān kept flying. Below it, Maināka remained. One had offered shelter; the other had received the offer and gone on. Neither had mistaken the other's purpose after they spoke.

Question: How did Hanumān show Maināka that he valued the offer, even though he could not stop?

Parent aside: The Sanskrit witness places the episode inside one long ocean-flight sarga. Maināka is a helper, not a divine obstacle or trap. His wing-history explains his gratitude to Vāyu. The closing line describes their actions; it does not claim Hanumān's refusal means rest is generally wrong.

If they ask — Did Hanumān rest on the mountain? No. He respectfully touched Maināka and continued his urgent crossing (5.1.131–136). The text treats the offered welcome and the onward journey as compatible.

Devī and the Messenger

Śumbha and Niśumbha had taken the gods' place in the heavens. The gods went to the Himalaya and called upon the Goddess who had promised to help them in distress.

Pārvatī came to the Gaṅgā to bathe. She asked whom they were praising. From her body emerged a radiant form, whom the text calls Kauśikī. The gods' hymn had found its answer.

Two followers of Śumbha saw the Goddess upon the mountain. They told him how brightly she shone. They also reminded him of the treasures he had gathered from the three worlds. Why, they asked, had he not taken her too?

Śumbha sent Sugrīva with a message. The messenger came to the Goddess and spoke of his master's power. The gods, he said, obeyed Śumbha. Their treasures belonged to him. The Goddess should come to him or his brother Niśumbha.

She answered that she had made a promise: she would marry only one who defeated her in battle and matched her strength. The messenger laughed at the thought that she could face the asuras. If she did not go willingly, he warned, she would be dragged away by her hair.

The Goddess did not change her answer. Tell your lord what I have said, she replied. Let him do what he thinks right.

The messenger went back. Angry at the reply, Śumbha sent a commander named Dhūmralocana with an army. His order was to bring her by force. When the commander reached her on the snowy mountain, he gave the same threat: come now, or be dragged away.

The Goddess saw the armed company before her. If you take me by force, what can I do? she asked. The commander rushed at her. She uttered a single huṃ. In that instant, he was reduced to ash.

The army was still there. The greater battle was still to come. But the first man sent to carry the Goddess away had not carried her anywhere.

Śumbha and his brother Niśumbha had seized the powers and places of the gods. Even the shares that belonged to the gods in sacred offerings were taken. Displaced from heaven, the gods remembered the Goddess. She had promised to help when they called to her in distress.

They went to the Himalaya and began to praise her. They called to the one present in every being: as awareness, as understanding, as hunger and thirst, as the power to act. Their hymn moved from one form to another. Wherever they looked for her, they found another way to name her.

Again and again, the gods offered their greeting to her presence in all beings. They named sleep, shadow, patience, and peace.

While they were praising, Pārvatī came to the Gaṅgā to bathe. She asked the gods whom they were calling. Then, from her body, a radiant form emerged. The hymn was for her. The text names this form Kauśikī.

Not far away, two followers of Śumbha, Caṇḍa and Muṇḍa, saw the Goddess upon the mountain. They went to their lord with news of her beauty. They spoke of her light reaching across the directions. Then they began to count what Śumbha already had. Airāvata, the elephant once belonging to Indra, was in his possession. So was the horse Uccaiḥśravas. He had the heavenly pārijāta tree, a swan-drawn chariot, jewels and other treasures.

The two followers asked why the Goddess had not been taken as another prize. That was how they saw her: one more thing their lord might own.

They even named the ocean's garland of lotuses and garments that Agni had given. In their report, everything had a place among Śumbha's possessions.

Śumbha sent a messenger named Sugrīva. The messenger found the Goddess seated on a beautiful part of the mountain and began with smooth words. His master ruled the three worlds, he said. The gods obeyed him. Śumbha held their offerings and their treasures. She should come to him. If she preferred, she could choose his brother Niśumbha instead. There would be riches with either one.

The Goddess listened. She did not deny that the brothers were powerful. But she had made a promise, she said. The one she married would have to defeat her in battle, overcome her pride, and equal her strength. The messenger should go back and tell the brothers to come if they wished to meet that condition.

The messenger's smooth voice gave way to a threat. Did she think she could stand against those asuras? He belittled her for being a woman. If she would not go willingly, he said, someone would drag her by her hair.

The Goddess still did not change her words. She told him to report everything she had said. Then Śumbha could decide what he thought right to do.

Sugrīva returned and gave his report. Śumbha was angry. He called for Dhūmralocana, a commander, and ordered him to take an army to the mountain. He was to bring the Goddess by force, dragging her by her hair. If anyone tried to protect her, he was to kill them too.

Dhūmralocana set out with sixty thousand asuras. On the snowy mountain he found the Goddess and shouted that she must come to Śumbha and Niśumbha. If she would not come willingly, he repeated, he would take her by force.

The Goddess answered him. He had been sent with an army, she said, and he was strong. If he took her by force, what could she do to him?

Dhūmralocana charged. Before he could seize her, the Goddess uttered one sound: huṃ. The commander became ash. His army had seen their leader run toward the one they had been told to carry away, and they had seen him disappear.

The battle had only begun. Here, on the snowy mountain, the messenger's boast and the commander's threat had both reached the Goddess. Her answer had not changed. No one had brought her to Śumbha.

தேவர்களுக்கு உரிய இடங்களையும் அதிகாரத்தையும் சும்பன், நிசும்பன் என்ற சகோதரர்கள் கைப்பற்றினர். யாகத்தில் தேவர்களுக்குச் சேர வேண்டிய பங்கும் அவர்களிடம் போயிற்று. எங்கே செல்வது என்று தவித்த தேவர்களுக்கு, தேவி முன்பு அளித்த வாக்கு நினைவுக்கு வந்தது. துன்பம் வந்தால் தன்னை அழைக்கலாம் என்று அவள் சொல்லியிருந்தாள்.

தேவர்கள் இமயமலைக்குச் சென்றார்கள். அங்கே நின்று தேவியைப் போற்றத் தொடங்கினார்கள். உயிர்களிடமெல்லாம் அறிவாக இருப்பவளே, தூக்கமாக இருப்பவளே, பசியாகவும் தாகமாகவும் இருப்பவளே என்று அழைத்தார்கள். நிழலிலும் அமைதியிலும் அவளை வணங்கினார்கள். ஒவ்வொரு முறையும் அதே வணக்கம் மீண்டும் ஒலித்தது.

அந்த நேரத்தில் கங்கையில் நீராட பார்வதி வந்தாள். தேவர்களைக் கண்டு, யாரைப் போற்றுகிறீர்கள்? என்று கேட்டாள். அவளுடைய உடலிலிருந்து ஒளி வீசும் ஒரு வடிவம் வெளிப்பட்டது. தேவர்களின் பாடல் அவளைத்தான் அழைத்தது. அந்த வடிவம் கௌசிகி என்று அழைக்கப்பட்டது. அவளிடம் மீதமிருந்த வடிவம் கருநிறம் கொண்டதாகி காளிகா என்று அழைக்கப்பட்டது.

மலையின் ஓர் இடத்தில் தேவியை சண்டன், முண்டன் என்ற இருவர் பார்த்தார்கள். அவர்கள் சும்பன் இருக்கும் இடத்துக்கு ஓடிச் சென்று அவள் ஒளியும் அழகும் பற்றிச் சொன்னார்கள். பிறகு அரசனிடம் இருந்த பொருட்களைப் பட்டியலிட்டார்கள். இந்திரன் முன்பு வைத்திருந்த ஐராவதம் யானை, உச்சைசிரவஸ் குதிரை, பாரிஜாத மரம், அன்னங்கள் இழுக்கும் வான ஊர்தி—எத்தனை அரியவை அவனிடம் இருந்தன! கடல் கொடுத்த வாடாத தாமரை மாலையையும் நினைவூட்டினார்கள்.

அந்தப் பட்டியலின் முடிவில் தேவியையும் சேர்த்தார்கள். மற்ற அரிய பொருட்களைப்போல அவளையும் அரசன் எடுத்துக்கொள்ளலாமே என்று கேட்டார்கள். அவர்கள் பேசிய விதம் அப்படித்தான். ஆனால் தேவியிடம் அவளுடைய பதில் இருந்தது.

சும்பன், சுக்ரீவன் என்ற தூதனை அனுப்பினான். தேவி அமர்ந்திருந்த மலைப்பகுதிக்குச் சென்ற தூதன் முதலில் இனிமையாகப் பேசினான். என் அரசன் மூன்று உலகங்களுக்கும் தலைவன். தேவர்கள் அவன் ஆணைக்குக் கீழ்ப்படிகிறார்கள். அவர்களுடைய யாகப் பங்கும் அவனுக்கே வருகிறது. அவர்களுடைய அரிய பொருட்களும் அவனிடம் இருக்கின்றன, என்றான்.

பின்னர் தான் வந்த காரியத்தைச் சொன்னான். தேவி சும்பன் இருக்கும் இடத்துக்கு வர வேண்டும்; அவள் விரும்பினால் நிசும்பன் என்பவரையும் தேர்ந்தெடுக்கலாம் என்றான். இருவரிடமும் செல்வம் குவிந்திருக்கிறது என்றும் சொன்னான். கேட்பதுபோல் இருந்தாலும், அவளுடைய விருப்பத்துக்கு அங்கே இடமே இல்லை.

தேவி அவனுக்கு அமைதியாகப் பதிலளித்தாள். சகோதரர்கள் வலிமையானவர்கள் என்பதை மறுக்கவில்லை. ஆனால் தான் முன்பு ஒரு உறுதி செய்துவிட்டதாகச் சொன்னாள். என்னைப் போரில் வென்று, என் ஆற்றலுக்கு இணையாக நிற்பவரையே நான் மணப்பேன். அவர்களிடம் போய் இதைச் சொல். அந்த நிபந்தனையை நிறைவேற்ற விரும்பினால் வரட்டும், என்றாள்.

தூதனின் இனிய பேச்சு மாறியது. பெண்ணான அவளால் அசுரர்களை எப்படி எதிர்க்க முடியும் என்று இகழ்ந்தான். விருப்பத்துடன் வராவிட்டால் தலைமுடியைப் பிடித்து இழுத்துச் செல்வார்கள் என மிரட்டினான். தேவி மீண்டும் தன் உறுதியையே சொன்னாள். நான் சொன்ன எல்லாவற்றையும் உன் அரசனிடம் தெரிவி. பிறகு அவன் தனக்குச் சரியென்று தோன்றுவதைச் செய்யட்டும், என்றாள்.

சுக்ரீவன் திரும்பிச் சென்று நடந்ததைச் சொன்னான். சும்பன் கோபமடைந்தான். தூம்ரலோசனன் என்ற படைத்தலைவனை அழைத்தான். தேவியைப் படையுடன் சென்று வலுக்கட்டாயமாகக் கொண்டுவர வேண்டும்; தலைமுடியைப் பிடித்து இழுக்க வேண்டும் என்று ஆணையிட்டான். அவளைக் காக்க யாராவது வந்தால் அவர்களையும் கொல்லச் சொன்னான்.

அறுபதாயிரம் அசுரர்களுடன் தூம்ரலோசனன் புறப்பட்டான். பனி படர்ந்த மலையில் அமர்ந்திருந்த தேவியைக் கண்டான். உடனே சும்பன் மற்றும் நிசும்பன் ஆகியோர் இருக்கும் இடத்துக்கு வா. இல்லாவிட்டால் இழுத்துச் செல்வேன், என்று கத்தினான்.

தேவி அவனைப் பார்த்தாள். நீ படையுடன் வந்திருக்கிறாய். வலிமையானவன். வலுக்கட்டாயமாக என்னை அழைத்துச் சென்றால் நான் என்ன செய்ய முடியும்? என்று கேட்டாள். அவன் அவளை நோக்கிப் பாய்ந்தான்.

தேவி ஒரு முறை ஹூம் என்று ஒலித்தாள். அவ்வளவுதான். தூம்ரலோசனன் சாம்பலானான். அவனுக்குப் பின்னால் வந்த படை இன்னும் அங்கேயே இருந்தது. தேவி மாகாத்மியம் அடுத்ததாகப் பெரிய போரைச் சொல்கிறது. ஆனால் இந்த முதல் மோதலில், அரசனின் தூதன் சொல்லையும் படைத்தலைவன் மிரட்டலையும் தேவி கேட்டாள். அவளை அழைத்துச் செல்ல யாராலும் முடியவில்லை.

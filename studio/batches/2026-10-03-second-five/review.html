Neither of Them Found the End

A column of fire takes in two terrible weapons. One god searches below, another flies above, and a falling flower is asked to witness something it never saw.

Parent and source notes

The two gods launch dangerous weapons. Near the end, an attendant seizes Brahmā’s fifth head and raises a sword, but Śiva stops him before the blow.

This follows the Vidyeśvara Saṃhitā, where Ketakī says it fell from the pillar’s middle and Śiva stops Bhairava before a blow. The Skanda Purāṇa has the flower claim an earlier place at the pillar’s crown; we keep the witnesses distinct.

Short telling 302 spoken words · timing pending

Brahmā and Viṣṇu were arguing over which of them was greater in the world. The quarrel grew into a battle. Each sent out a terrible weapon, and the gods feared that the three worlds would burn.

The gods went to Śiva for help. When he reached the battlefield, the noise of his attendants stopped. The two weapons were still flying toward each other. Then the fire rose between them.

Then a column of fire appeared between them. Both weapons vanished into it. It rose beyond sight and went down beyond sight.

What is this? they wondered. Can either of us find its end?

· · ·

Viṣṇu took the form of a boar and went below. Brahmā became a swan and flew upward. Far down, Viṣṇu found no bottom. He returned and said so.

Far above, Brahmā saw a Ketakī flower falling through the fire. It told him that it had been falling for years, yet it had not come from the top. Brahmā asked the flower to say it had seen him reach the top anyway.

· · ·

When the two met again, Viṣṇu admitted he had failed. Brahmā claimed he had succeeded. The flower backed his claim.

Śiva stepped out of the fire. The pillar had been his form. He praised Viṣṇu for speaking truth even when he wanted to win. Brahmā's false claim could no longer stand.

Viṣṇu asked Śiva to forgive him. Śiva did spare Brahmā, but said he would not have his own temples and festivals. The flower, too, was barred from Śiva's worship. Śiva later allowed it to decorate the canopy above his image.

Two gods went looking for the end of a fire without an end. One returned without an answer. The other returned with an answer he had not found. Only the first could stand by what he said.

Brahmā came upon Viṣṇu resting on his serpent couch. He wanted Viṣṇu to rise and honour him. Viṣṇu welcomed him, but neither accepted the other's claim to be the greater lord.

Their words became a battle. They fought from their vehicles, a swan and Garuḍa, while their attendants joined in. The gods watched from above. Then Brahmā and Viṣṇu launched weapons with fire fierce enough to threaten all three worlds.

The frightened gods went to Śiva. He came to the battlefield, and the music and shouting of his attendants fell silent. Between the two burning weapons, a column of fire suddenly rose. Both weapons disappeared into it.

The gods had found Śiva seated with Pārvatī. He knew why they had come. When he reached the place of battle, he made himself unseen. Only the new fire between Brahmā and Viṣṇu showed what he had done.

· · ·

Brahmā and Viṣṇu looked up. They could not see where the fire ended. They looked down. They could not see where it began.

We must find its top and bottom, they decided. They would search in opposite directions.

Viṣṇu became a boar and went down through the worlds below. Brahmā became a swan and flew upward. The boar kept going, farther and farther, until he was exhausted. No root appeared. He turned back.

The swan flew high for a long time. Then Brahmā saw a Ketakī flower drifting downward through the fire. Its fragrance and colour were still fresh.

The text says the flower had been falling for years, yet its scent and brightness had not faded. It was no ordinary petal blown loose from a tree. Brahmā stopped his climb to speak to it instead of continuing toward a top he still could not see.

Brahmā asked where it had come from. The flower said it had fallen from the middle of the column and had been falling for years. If even the flower had not seen the top, how could Brahmā hope to find it?

· · ·

Brahmā asked the flower to help him with another answer. When they returned, it should tell Viṣṇu that Brahmā had reached the top and that the flower had witnessed it. The flower agreed.

At the place where they had begun, Viṣṇu admitted he had searched below and found no bottom. Brahmā said he had seen the top. The flower supported him. Viṣṇu believed them and honoured Brahmā.

Then Śiva stepped out of the column. It had been his own form, beyond the ends either god could find. Viṣṇu bowed and asked forgiveness for their quarrel and their search.

Śiva told Viṣṇu that he was pleased with him. Viṣṇu had wanted to be first, yet he had spoken truly about his failure. Śiva promised him honour and worship.

· · ·

Śiva turned to Brahmā. The claim about reaching the top had been false, and the flower had repeated it. Śiva summoned Bhairava, who seized Brahmā's fifth head and raised a sword. Brahmā fell at his feet. Viṣṇu pleaded for him, and Śiva told Bhairava to stop.

For the parent, not read aloud

The text includes a threatened blow, not a beheading in this scene. Let the parent decide whether to pause here with a younger listener.

Śiva said Brahmā would not have his own temples and festivals. Yet he also gave Brahmā a place in sacred rites. A sacrifice would need him. Sparing Brahmā was not the same as calling the lie true.

Brahmā accepted that his head had been spared. Śiva did not leave the creator without a duty; he made Brahmā necessary at household and public sacrifices. Both the mercy and the consequence remained in force.

Śiva then told the Ketakī flower it could not be offered in his worship. When the flower asked for mercy, he allowed his followers to wear it and allowed it above his image as a canopy decoration.

Viṣṇu came back empty-handed and told the truth. Brahmā came back with a witness and a claim. The column of fire had no end for either of them, but the words each chose at the end were entirely his own.

After the story

If you had searched as far as you could and found nothing, what would make it hard to say so?

The truth matters most when a better answer would win you praise.

Why did Śiva appear as a column of fire?

In this Śiva Purāṇa passage, the weapons of Brahmā and Viṣṇu threaten the three worlds. Śiva takes a form that receives the weapons and has no end they can find. The search lets them discover that neither can claim to have measured him.

Did Śiva cut off Brahmā’s head?

Not in the Vidyeśvara Saṃhitā passage we tell. Bhairava seizes Brahmā’s fifth head and prepares to strike, but Viṣṇu asks for mercy and Śiva stops him. Some other Śaiva tellings describe a severing; that is a different telling and should not be slipped into this one.

Why did the flower agree to lie?

The text has Brahmā ask it to witness a claim it could not know. It does not give us the flower’s private thoughts. What it does show is that Śiva holds the flower responsible for repeating the false claim, then allows it a smaller place near his worship after it asks forgiveness.

फूल की गवाही

अग्नि का स्तंभ था, जिसका छोर किसी को नहीं मिला। फिर केतकी के फूल ने वह बात कही जो उसने देखी ही नहीं थी।

Parent and source notes

इस कथा में युद्ध के अस्त्र और बाद में उठी तलवार का प्रसंग है। तलवार का प्रहार नहीं होता। बच्चे को पहले बता दें कि भैरव को शिव रोक देते हैं।

यह पाठ शिवपुराण की विद्येश्वर संहिता के प्रसंग का अनुसरण करता है। यहाँ केतकी फूल स्तंभ के बीच से गिरने की बात कहता है; स्कंदपुराण के एक पाठ में फूल की कथा अलग है। ब्रह्मा का सिर कटने की दूसरी कथाओं को यहाँ नहीं मिलाया गया है।

ब्रह्मा और विष्णु में इस बात पर झगड़ा हुआ कि दोनों में बड़ा कौन है। बात इतनी बढ़ी कि युद्ध छिड़ गया। दोनों ने ऐसे भयंकर अस्त्र छोड़े जिनकी आग से तीनों लोकों को संकट हो सकता था। देवता घबराकर शिव के पास पहुँचे।

शिव युद्धभूमि में आए। वहाँ अचानक आग का एक विराट स्तंभ प्रकट हुआ। दोनों अस्त्र उसमें समा गए। ब्रह्मा और विष्णु ऊपर देखते रहे, पर स्तंभ का शिखर दिखाई नहीं दिया। नीचे झाँका तो उसकी जड़ भी कहीं नहीं दिखी।

· · ·

दोनों ने तय किया कि एक ऊपर जाएगा और दूसरा नीचे। विष्णु वराह का रूप लेकर धरती के नीचे उतरते चले गए। ब्रह्मा हंस बनकर आकाश की ओर उड़ चले। बहुत दूर जाने पर भी विष्णु को स्तंभ का तल नहीं मिला। थककर वे वापस लौट आए।

ऊपर उड़ते हुए ब्रह्मा को केतकी का एक फूल नीचे गिरता दिखाई दिया। फूल ने बताया कि वह स्तंभ के बीच से गिरा है और वर्षों से गिरता आ रहा है। उसे भी शिखर का पता नहीं था। फिर भी ब्रह्मा ने उससे कहा कि लौटकर विष्णु के सामने गवाही देना—मैं ऊपर तक पहुँच गया था।

· · ·

विष्णु ने लौटकर साफ कहा कि उन्हें जड़ नहीं मिली। ब्रह्मा ने कहा कि उन्होंने शिखर देख लिया है। फूल ने उनकी बात दोहरा दी। विष्णु ने उस गवाही पर भरोसा किया और ब्रह्मा का सम्मान किया।

तभी शिव उसी अग्नि-स्तंभ से प्रकट हुए। उन्होंने विष्णु की सच्चाई की सराहना की। ब्रह्मा और फूल की बात झूठी थी; आग का कोई छोर उन्हें नहीं मिला था।

विष्णु ने ब्रह्मा के लिए क्षमा माँगी। शिव ने उन्हें बचा लिया, लेकिन कहा कि ब्रह्मा के अपने मंदिर और उत्सव नहीं होंगे। केतकी को भी उनकी पूजा में चढ़ाने से रोका गया; बाद में उसे मूर्ति के ऊपर सजाने की अनुमति मिली।

दोनों खाली हाथ लौटे थे। फर्क इतना था कि एक ने वैसा ही कहा, और दूसरे ने फूल से अपने लिए एक झूठी गवाही दिलवा दी।

एक बार ब्रह्मा ने विष्णु को विश्राम करते देखा। वे चाहते थे कि विष्णु उठकर उनका आदर करें। विष्णु ने उनका स्वागत किया, लेकिन दोनों में यह बात उलझ गई कि सबसे बड़ा कौन है। दोनों ही अपने को प्रधान मानते थे।

बातों का झगड़ा युद्ध में बदल गया। ब्रह्मा हंस पर थे और विष्णु गरुड़ पर। उनके साथ आए गण भी भिड़े। देवता ऊपर से देख रहे थे। फिर दोनों ओर से ऐसे प्रचंड अस्त्र चले जिनकी लपटें तीनों लोकों को जला सकती थीं।

देवता सहायता माँगने शिव के पास गए। वे पार्वती के साथ बैठे थे और झगड़े का समाचार उन्हें पहले से था। वे युद्धभूमि में पहुँचे। उनके साथियों का शोर थम गया। दो जलते अस्त्र अब भी एक दूसरे की ओर बढ़ रहे थे।

· · ·

उसी क्षण बीच में अग्नि का एक विशाल स्तंभ उठ खड़ा हुआ। दोनों अस्त्र उसमें समा गए। ब्रह्मा और विष्णु ने उसे ऊपर तक देखा, फिर नीचे तक; किसी ओर उसका अंत दिखाई नहीं देता था। जो आग विनाश की ओर बढ़ रही थी, वह इस आग में बुझ गई थी।

दोनों ने सोचा कि इस स्तंभ का छोर ढूँढ़ा जाए। साथ चलेंगे तो खोज का क्या अर्थ होगा? इसलिए रास्ते अलग किए। विष्णु ने वराह का रूप लिया और नीचे उतरने लगे। ब्रह्मा हंस बनकर ऊपर उड़े।

विष्णु नीचे के लोकों को पार करते गए। जितना आगे गए, स्तंभ की जड़ उतनी ही दूर रही। थककर वे लौटे। उन्हें कोई तल नहीं मिला था। यह बात मानना आसान नहीं था, क्योंकि अभी कुछ ही देर पहले वे अपने को सबसे बड़ा कह रहे थे। फिर भी वे जो देख पाए थे, वही कहने वाले थे।

· · ·

उधर ब्रह्मा ऊपर उड़ते रहे। उन्हें एक केतकी फूल नीचे गिरता दिखा। फूल की सुगंध और रंग अब भी ताज़ा थे, हालाँकि वह वर्षों से गिरता आ रहा था। ब्रह्मा ने पूछा कि वह कहाँ से आया है। फूल ने कहा—स्तंभ के बीच से। शिखर उसने भी नहीं देखा था।

अब ब्रह्मा के सामने दो रास्ते थे। वे लौटकर कह सकते थे कि उनकी उड़ान भी व्यर्थ रही। लेकिन उन्होंने फूल से दूसरी बात कहलवाने को कहा। विष्णु के सामने वह कहे कि ब्रह्मा शिखर तक पहुँचे थे और फूल इसका साक्षी है। फूल मान गया।

दोनों फिर उसी जगह मिले। विष्णु ने कहा कि उन्हें नीचे की जड़ नहीं मिली। ब्रह्मा ने कहा कि उन्हें ऊपर का छोर मिल गया। केतकी ने भी वही बात कही। विष्णु ने भरोसा किया और ब्रह्मा को आदर दिया। एक फूल की गवाही ने असफल खोज को जीत जैसा दिखा दिया।

· · ·

तभी उस अग्नि-स्तंभ से शिव प्रकट हुए। स्तंभ उन्हीं का रूप था। वे जानते थे कि कौन कितना दूर गया और किसने क्या देखा। विष्णु ने उनके चरण पकड़े और अपने गर्व तथा झगड़े के लिए क्षमा माँगी।

शिव ने विष्णु से कहा कि अपने को बड़ा सिद्ध करने की चाह के बीच भी उन्होंने सत्य नहीं छोड़ा। विष्णु को उन्होंने सम्मान और पूजा का स्थान दिया। अब ब्रह्मा की ओर देखा गया। फूल की गवाही उनके काम नहीं आ सकती थी।

शिव ने भैरव को बुलाया। उन्होंने ब्रह्मा का पाँचवाँ सिर पकड़ा और तलवार उठाई। ब्रह्मा भय से झुक गए। विष्णु ने उनके लिए विनती की। शिव ने भैरव को रोक दिया। इस प्रसंग में प्रहार नहीं होता; बच जाने पर भी झूठ का परिणाम बना रहता है।

शिव ने कहा कि ब्रह्मा के अलग मंदिर और उत्सव नहीं होंगे। साथ ही यज्ञों में उनका स्थान रहेगा; उनके बिना वे पूरे नहीं होंगे। ब्रह्मा ने सिर बच जाने को ही बड़ी कृपा माना।

केतकी को भी पूजा में चढ़ाने से रोक दिया गया। फूल ने दया माँगी। तब शिव ने कहा कि उनके अनुयायी उसे धारण कर सकते हैं और उनकी मूर्ति के ऊपर की सजावट में वह रह सकता है। एक झूठी बात से फूल का पूरा अस्तित्व मिटाया नहीं गया, पर पूजा में उसका स्थान बदल गया।

विष्णु खाली हाथ लौटे और सत्य कहा। ब्रह्मा भी खाली हाथ लौटे, लेकिन एक फूल को साक्षी बनाकर वह उपलब्धि बता दी जो हुई ही नहीं थी।

After the story

अगर बहुत खोजने पर भी कोई उत्तर न मिले, तो सच कहने में सबसे मुश्किल क्या होगा?

जो नहीं मिला, उसे मिल जाने का दावा न करना भी साहस है।

स्तंभ कहाँ से आया था?

शिवपुराण के इस प्रसंग में शिव स्वयं अग्नि-स्तंभ के रूप में प्रकट हुए। दोनों भयंकर अस्त्र उसमें समा गए; फिर ब्रह्मा और विष्णु उसके छोर खोजने निकले।

क्या ब्रह्मा का सिर काट दिया गया?

इस चुने हुए पाठ में नहीं। भैरव तलवार उठाते हैं, लेकिन विष्णु के अनुरोध पर शिव उन्हें रोक देते हैं। दूसरी कथाओं का अलग प्रसंग यहाँ नहीं जोड़ा गया है।

Bhagīratha Waits for the River

The river is caught in Śiva’s hair, then swallowed by a sage. A king keeps going because sixty thousand ancestors still wait for her water.

Parent and source notes

The story begins with the ashes of sixty thousand ancestors and ends when the river’s water releases them. Their deaths are recounted, not shown.

This telling follows Vālmīki’s Bāla Kāṇḍa, including Gaṅgā’s confinement in Śiva’s hair and the interruption at Jahnu’s rite. Purāṇic accounts also tell her descent, but the scene sequence and claims here are from the Rāmāyaṇa.

The ashes of King Sagara's sixty thousand sons lay far below the earth. Their descendant Aṃśumān had wanted to bring Gaṅgā to them. His son Dilīpa had wanted it too. Neither lived to see the river descend.

Then Dilīpa's son Bhagīratha became king. He entrusted his kingdom to his ministers and went to practise tapas, the long, hard work of prayer and self-discipline.

Brahmā came to him and offered a boon. Bhagīratha asked for Gaṅgā's water to touch his ancestors' ashes, so they could reach heaven.

Brahmā agreed, but said the earth could not bear the river's fall from the sky. Only Śiva could catch her.

Bhagīratha prayed to Śiva. Śiva agreed to hold Gaṅgā on his head.

She came down with tremendous force. Śiva caught her in his matted hair, where she could find no way out. Bhagīratha prayed again. At last Śiva released her, and one stream followed Bhagīratha's chariot.

The river ran swiftly, with fish and other water creatures tumbling in her current. Then she flooded the place where the sage Jahnu was making an offering.

Long before Bhagīratha was born, King Sagara's sixty thousand sons had died far below the earth. Their ashes remained there. Water from Gaṅgā could bring them release, but she flowed above the earth, beyond their reach.

Sagara's descendant Aṃśumān learned what was needed. He hoped to bring the river down but died before he could. His son Dilīpa kept the task before him. He too died without seeing Gaṅgā descend. Then Dilīpa's son Bhagīratha inherited a kingdom and an unfinished duty.

He left the kingdom in the care of his ministers and went to Gokarṇa. There he stood with his arms raised and practised tapas for a long time, asking for the strength to finish what his father and grandfather could not.

Bhagīratha had no child then, and the old duty was not the only thing on his mind. He wanted the family line to continue. Still, his first journey was away from the palace, to the place where he could ask for help with the river.

· · ·

Brahmā came with the gods and invited him to ask for a boon. Bhagīratha joined his hands. He asked for Gaṅgā's water to reach Sagara's sons. When it touched their ashes, they could go to heaven. He also asked that his family line continue.

Brahmā agreed. Then he told Bhagīratha the next difficulty. Gaṅgā would fall from the sky with such force that the earth could not endure it. Only Śiva could receive her. One answer had brought Bhagīratha to a new task.

So he prayed to Śiva. After a year, Śiva appeared and promised to bear Gaṅgā on his head.

Gaṅgā rushed downward. Śiva caught her in his matted hair. The river turned through the coils but could not find an opening. Years passed. Bhagīratha saw that the water still had not reached the earth, and he prayed again.

· · ·

Śiva released her into a lake. Six streams went east and west; a seventh followed Bhagīratha. He rode ahead in his chariot, and Gaṅgā came after him.

The water was full of life. Fish and other water creatures fell with it. People and gods gathered to watch. In one place the river ran straight; in another it bent; elsewhere it leapt upward and fell again. Bhagīratha kept moving, because the ashes were still far away.

The sky shone with the ornaments of gods who came to watch. The river foamed white as she descended. Some people touched her water where it fell from Śiva’s head; Bhagīratha, however, could not stop at the first place where she reached earth.

The river reached the place where the sage Jahnu was performing a rite. Her water flooded his offering ground. Angry, Jahnu drank the whole river. Once more Bhagīratha's path had no Gaṅgā in it.

The gods and sages honoured Jahnu and asked him to release her. He did. Gaṅgā flowed from his ears, and from that day she was called Jāhnavī, the daughter of Jahnu. She went on behind Bhagīratha's chariot.

· · ·

They reached the ocean, then the world below it. Bhagīratha saw the heap of ashes and was grieved. Gaṅgā's water covered the remains of Sagara's sons. The Rāmāyaṇa says they were freed and went to heaven.

Brahmā came again. He told Bhagīratha that Aṃśumān and Dilīpa had longed to do this but could not. He said Gaṅgā would bear Bhagīratha's name, Bhāgīrathī. Bhagīratha then offered water for his ancestors as a family member does, and returned to his city. His people welcomed their king home.

Brahmā called her Bhāgīrathī because Bhagīratha had brought her down. He also named her as a river of three paths, through heaven, earth and the world below. The name remembered a journey, not simply the king who asked for it.

Aṃśumān could not finish it. Dilīpa could not finish it. Bhagīratha inherited their wish, found a path for the river, and stayed with her until that path reached its end.

Finishing can mean carrying an old promise through every new obstacle.

Why did Gaṅgā have to land on Śiva’s head?

Brahmā tells Bhagīratha that the earth could not withstand her fall from the sky. Śiva agrees to receive her. In this Rāmāyaṇa passage she remains caught in his matted hair until Bhagīratha prays again and Śiva releases a stream.

Why did Jahnu drink the whole river?

Gaṅgā floods the place where he is performing a sacred rite, and he becomes angry. The gods and sages ask him to release her. He does, through his ears, which is why this passage gives her the name Jāhnavī.

Did Bhagīratha do what Aṃśumān and Dilīpa could not?

Yes. The Rāmāyaṇa says both earlier kings wished to bring Gaṅgā down, but neither completed it. Bhagīratha asked Brahmā and Śiva for help, continued when Gaṅgā was caught in Śiva’s hair, and led her to the ashes below the ocean.

Two Lights in the Anthill

Sukanyā touches two lights in an anthill with thorns. Only afterward does she learn who was inside.

Parent and source notes

Sukanyā unknowingly injures a sage's eyes. He then demands that her father give her to him in marriage before he will forgive the family. The telling keeps that pressure visible; it does not call the marriage her free choice.

We follow the Mahābhārata: Sukanyā identifies Cyavana among three identical young men. The Bhāgavata Purāṇa tells this differently: she cannot distinguish him, and the Aśvins show her which man is her husband.

For so long had the sage Cyavana sat beside a lake in prayer that an anthill had grown around him. Ants moved across the earth and creepers covered the mound. From inside it, only his eyes could be seen.

King Śaryāti came to the lake with his daughter Sukanyā and his household. Walking among the flowering trees, she noticed two little lights in the earth. The sage called to her, but his voice was too low for her to hear.

What are those? she wondered. She put thorns into the two bright places. They were not lights at all. They were the eyes of the man inside the anthill.

· · ·

Cyavana was hurt and angry. The king's men were suddenly unable to relieve themselves. No one knew why. When her father asked what had happened near the sage, Sukanyā saw the men's distress and told him what she had done.

The king went to the anthill and asked Cyavana to forgive her. The sage said he would do so only if Sukanyā became his wife. Her father agreed without waiting. No one asked her to choose. She went to live with Cyavana and cared for him, their guests, and the sacred fire.

The sage Cyavana sat beside a lake and gave himself to long austerities. He stayed in one place so long that ants built up earth around him. Creepers grew over the mound. A person walking past might have seen only an anthill, not a man still sitting inside it.

One day King Śaryāti came to that lake with his household. His daughter Sukanyā walked among the trees with her companions, looking at the forest and breaking small flowering twigs. She came close to the mound.

From within the mound Cyavana saw her. He called out, but his voice was very low, and she did not hear him. Instead she noticed two bright points of light shining out of the earth.

She wondered what they were. In the Mahābhārata she later tells her father that she had thought they might be glowworms. She took thorns and pierced both lights. They were the sage's eyes.

· · ·

Cyavana felt great pain. His anger reached the king's men: they found they could no longer pass water or relieve themselves. They were distressed, and the king knew that something had gone wrong near the sage's dwelling.

He questioned the people with him. Had anyone harmed Cyavana? No one could explain what had happened. Sukanyā saw her father's worry and the trouble among the soldiers. She told him about the two lights and the thorns.

The king went straight to the anthill. With his hands joined, he asked the old sage to forgive the harm his daughter had done without knowing. Cyavana named his condition: he wanted Sukanyā as his bride. Only then, he said, would he forgive them.

Śaryāti gave his daughter to the sage without waiting. No one asked Sukanyā to agree or refuse. The king's party returned home; Sukanyā stayed beside the lake.

She tended Cyavana. She also welcomed guests and cared for the sacred fire. Time passed as they lived in the forest.

· · ·

One day the Aśvins saw Sukanyā. They were twin physicians of the gods. When they learned whose wife she was, they asked why she remained with an old man. They urged her to leave him and take one of them instead.

Sukanyā answered plainly: she was devoted to Cyavana. The twins did not stop there. They offered another path. They could make the sage young; afterward she could choose among him and the two of them.

She went to Cyavana and repeated the offer. He told her to accept it. So she returned to the twins. They called the sage to the lake, and all three entered the water together.

When they came out, three young men stood before her. All wore shining earrings. All looked alike. The husband she had known as old and worn was somewhere among them, but no longer had the old face by which she knew him.

The twins told her to choose the one she wished for a husband. This time the story pauses with Sukanyā looking. She considered the three men, worked out which one was Cyavana, and chose him.

· · ·

Cyavana was pleased. In thanks, he promised the divine physicians a share of soma, the sacred drink they had not been allowed to receive at sacrifices. The twins were delighted and went back to heaven. Cyavana and Sukanyā stayed in the forest together.

The two choices in this tale are not the same. At the anthill, she reaches toward something before she knows what it is. Her father then settles her future under the sage's demand.

At the lake, with the twins waiting, she is asked to look and choose for herself. The three men stand alike before her; no old face makes the answer easy now.

Sukanyā could not take back the thorns, and she had not chosen the bargain her father made. But when three figures rose from the water, she took the time to see which one she would name.

After the story

Which choice in Sukanyā's story feels most like her own, and why?

Looking closely can change what we choose, even when we cannot change what came before.

Did Sukanyā want to marry the sage?

The Mahābhārata says Cyavana demanded the marriage as the condition for forgiving her father, and the king gave her to him. It does not describe her consent at that moment. We should not turn that silence into a cheerful answer. Later, when the Aśvins offered her another choice, the text says she chose Cyavana.

How did she know which young man was Cyavana?

Ganguli's Mahābhārata translation says she considered the three identical men and recognized her husband, but it does not explain the sign she used. The Bhāgavata Purāṇa tells this differently: she could not distinguish them, and the Aśvins showed her Cyavana.

Were his eyes healed?

The chosen passage tells us that the thorns hurt Cyavana's eyes and later that the Aśvins made him young in the lake. It does not describe a separate eye-healing scene, so we do not add one.

उन्होंने अपने लोगों से पूछा। किसी के पास उत्तर नहीं था। सुकन्या ने पिता की चिंता और सैनिकों की हालत देखी। तब उन्होंने छिपाया नहीं। उन्होंने बताया कि टीले में दो रोशनी दिखी थीं और उन्होंने काँटों से उन्हें छेदा था।

राजा तुरंत ऋषि के पास गए। हाथ जोड़कर कहा कि पुत्री ने जान-बूझकर हानि नहीं पहुँचाई; उन्हें क्षमा दें। च्यवन ने अपनी शर्त रखी। यदि सुकन्या उनकी पत्नी बनें, तभी वे क्षमा करेंगे और सैनिकों का कष्ट हटेगा।

शर्याति ने यह शर्त मान ली। महाभारत उस क्षण सुकन्या की सहमति या असहमति के शब्द नहीं देता। हम उस चुप्पी को उनकी प्रसन्न स्वीकृति नहीं बना सकते। पिता और ऋषि ने निर्णय किया; सुकन्या वहीं झील के पास रहने लगीं।

उन्होंने च्यवन की सेवा की। आने वाले अतिथियों का सत्कार किया और यज्ञ की अग्नि सँभाली। दिन बीतते गए। जिस भूल से यह जीवन शुरू हुआ था, वह मिट नहीं सकती थी, लेकिन वह पूरी जीवन-कथा भी नहीं थी। आगे उनके सामने एक अलग चुनाव आने वाला था।

जुड़वाँ वैद्यों ने दूसरा प्रस्ताव रखा। वे च्यवन को युवा बना सकते थे। उसके बाद तीनों युवक एक साथ खड़े होंगे और सुकन्या अपनी इच्छा से चुनेंगी। सुकन्या यह बात ऋषि तक ले गईं। च्यवन ने प्रस्ताव स्वीकार किया।

तीनों झील में उतरे—दो अश्विनी कुमार और च्यवन। जब वे पानी से निकले तो तीनों की उम्र और रूप एक-जैसे लगते थे। सबके कानों में चमकते कुंडल थे। पुराना चेहरा, जिसे देखकर सुकन्या पति को पहचानती थीं, अब वैसा नहीं रहा।

जुड़वाँ भाइयों ने कहा कि तीनों में से पति चुन लो। सुकन्या ने जल्दबाज़ी नहीं की। उन्होंने तीनों को ध्यान से देखा। महाभारत कहता है कि उन्होंने च्यवन को पहचान लिया और उन्हीं को चुना। पहचान का कोई गुप्त चिह्न वह पाठ नहीं बताता।

कथा में दो अलग क्षण साथ-साथ रखे हैं। टीले के पास सुकन्या ने बिना जाने काँटे बढ़ाए थे। उसके बाद पिता ने उनके लिए विवाह का निर्णय लिया, जिसकी सहमति पाठ नहीं दिखाता।

झील के पास परिस्थिति दूसरी थी। तीन लोग सामने खड़े थे, सबका रूप लगभग एक-सा था। यहाँ उन्हें देखना, पहचानना और चुनना था। पहली भूल को वह वापस नहीं ले सकती थीं; फिर भी बाद के चुनाव को उनकी अपनी दृष्टि से अलग नहीं किया जा सकता।

जिसने कभी दो चमकती बिंदियों को समझे बिना छू लिया था, उसी सुकन्या ने अब तीन चेहरों को पूरा देखकर अपना उत्तर दिया।

सुकन्या की कथा में कौन-सा चुनाव सचमुच उनका अपना लगता है, और क्यों?

किसी को ध्यान से देखने पर हमारा चुनाव बदल सकता है; पहले हुई बात हम हमेशा बदल नहीं सकते।

क्या सुकन्या ऋषि से विवाह करना चाहती थीं?

महाभारत कहता है कि ऋषि ने क्षमा के बदले विवाह माँगा और पिता ने बात मान ली। उस क्षण सुकन्या की सहमति का वर्णन नहीं है। बाद में अश्विनी कुमारों के सामने चुनने का अवसर आता है और वह च्यवन को चुनती हैं।

तीन एक-जैसे युवकों में उन्होंने च्यवन को कैसे पहचाना?

चुने हुए महाभारत पाठ में केवल इतना है कि उन्होंने ध्यान से देखकर पति को पहचान लिया। कोई निशान या उपाय नहीं बताया गया। भागवत पुराण का विवरण अलग है; उसे यहाँ नहीं जोड़ते।

क्या ऋषि की आँखें ठीक हुई थीं?

पाठ बताता है कि काँटों से आँखों को चोट लगी और बाद में अश्विनी कुमारों ने झील में उन्हें युवा बनाया। आँखें ठीक करने का अलग दृश्य वह नहीं देता; इसलिए हमने भी नहीं जोड़ा।

The Jar Above the Cot

A brahmin dreams that a jar of barley-meal will become a house, a family and a fortune. His foot reaches the jar first.

Parent and source notes

In a daydream, the brahmin imagines kicking his future wife when she does not respond to him. No wife or child exists in the scene, and the actual kick breaks his own jar. The telling does not present violence toward her as a joke or a solution.

This telling follows Ryder's Pūrṇabhadra text: the jar holds barley-meal and hangs above the brahmin's cot. Retellings that say rice or put the pot on a shelf are not the witness used here.

Two travellers in the Pañcatantra were talking about hopes that grow too large. One told the other of a poor brahmin named Svabhāvakṛpaṇa.

He received barley-meal by begging, ate some and kept the rest in a jar. One night he hung the precious jar from a peg and put his cot beneath it. As he looked up at the barley-meal he had saved, his thoughts wandered far beyond that room.

If famine comes, he thought, I can sell this meal for a hundred rupees. Then I can buy two she-goats. He imagined their young growing into a herd. When the herd was big enough, he would sell it and buy cows.

The cows would have calves, and selling those calves would bring buffaloes. Buffaloes would lead to mares. The mares would bear horses, and the horses would bring him gold. In his mind he already had a great house with an inner court, although he had not yet sold a spoonful of meal.

· · ·

Then he imagined a wife and a son. He would call the boy Moon-Lord. The brahmin pictured himself reading on the stable roof. The child would rush from his mother's lap toward him, close to the horses' hooves.

In the dream the brahmin called to his wife to take the child away. She was busy and did not answer. He imagined growing angry enough to get up and kick her.

· · ·

His foot moved for real. It struck the jar hanging above him. The jar broke, and all the barley-meal fell over him, turning him white. There were no goats, horses, great house, wife or child in that room. There was only a broken jar and the food he had saved.

The first thing he had actually owned was the very first thing his daydream cost him that night.

In the Pañcatantra, two travellers have been arguing about wanting more than one can safely reach. One tells the other about a brahmin who had not yet lost anything when his mind began to spend it.

The brahmin, Svabhāvakṛpaṇa, lived on what he could obtain by begging. One day he had barley-meal. He ate a portion and filled a jar with what remained.

He hung the jar on a peg. Beneath it he placed his cot, so that when he lay down the meal was above him. He could see it from where he rested. That night he looked up at the jar and began to imagine what it might become.

If there is a famine, he thought, people will need food. I will sell the barley-meal for a hundred rupees. No famine had come. He had not sold the meal. Yet in his mind he was already holding the money.

With that money I will buy two she-goats. He imagined the goats bearing young. More goats would follow, and after enough births he would have a whole herd. He could then sell the herd and buy cows.

· · ·

The cows would have calves. Selling the calves would let him buy buffaloes. Then he would trade again and buy mares. Each new animal existed only because he had already imagined selling the one before it.

The mares would bear horses. Horses would bring plenty of gold. He no longer looked at the jar as food for another day; in his thoughts it had become a long line of animals, each one turning into a larger purchase.

With the gold he would buy a great house. The Pañcatantra gives it an inner court. His dream had moved from a cot beneath one hanging jar to a home large enough for a family he did not yet have.

He imagined that someone would offer him a daughter in marriage. In the dream he married her, and they had a son. He even chose the boy's name: Moon-Lord.

The boy would grow old enough to ride on his father's knee. The brahmin pictured taking a book up to the stable roof and sitting there to read. The child, eager to reach him, would leave his mother's lap and come near the horses.

· · ·

Now the imagined horses had hooves close to an imagined child. The brahmin thought he would call to his wife to take the boy away. In his dream she was busy with her work and did not heed him.

He imagined becoming angry. Rather than going to the child himself, he pictured rising and kicking his wife. This was not a wise or kind answer even inside the dream. It was the act his mind reached for while his real body lay on the cot.

The brahmin's leg moved. He kicked upward at the jar above him. It smashed. The barley-meal he had begged, eaten from and saved came down over him. It covered him so thoroughly that he was white all over.

· · ·

He had not sold a single mouthful. He had not bought goats, cows, buffaloes or mares. There were no horses outside a stable and no gold waiting in a house. The wife and son had been people in a daydream, not people standing beside him.

Only the jar and its contents had been real, and they were broken and scattered. The chain of trades had needed a famine, a buyer, growing herds and one successful sale after another. His mind had stepped over all those uncertain parts as if they had already happened.

The traveller who tells the tale uses it to warn his companion against extravagant hope. The brahmin's loss comes at the exact point when the action in his dream reaches his sleeping body: he kicks, and the food on which the entire dream depended is gone.

Svabhāvakṛpaṇa had built a whole life out of one jar. Before any part of that life arrived, he kicked away the one thing he truly had.

After the story

When did the brahmin's plan stop being a plan and start becoming a dream he believed had already happened?

A plan can help, but we must remember which parts have happened and which are only hoped for.

Was it rice in the pot?

In the Ryder translation of the Pūrṇabhadra Pañcatantra used here, it was barley-meal. He got it by begging, ate some and saved the rest in a jar hung above his cot. Other retellings change the food, so the source named on this page matters.

Did he really kick his wife?

No wife appears in the room. He imagined a future wife not responding when their imagined son went near imagined horses. Then his real foot kicked the jar. The idea of kicking a person is still harmful; we do not need to laugh at her to understand why the pot breaks.

Is it wrong to imagine a better future?

The Pañcatantra's warning is about treating a hoped-for chain of events as though every step is already secure. Imagining possibilities is different from forgetting what is in front of you. The brahmin could have kept the barley-meal and still hoped; his unexamined action destroyed what he had.

When Rāvaṇa Shook Kailāsa

A flying chariot stops at Śiva’s mountain. Rāvaṇa decides to move the mountain itself, until one toe presses down.

Rāvaṇa's flying chariot had carried him past many places. Near Mount Kailāsa, it stopped. No matter how he urged it forward, Puṣpaka would not move. The king who had seized that chariot from his brother was not used to being told where he could go.

Nandī, an attendant of Śiva, came to meet him. Śiva was on this mountain, Nandī said. No one could pass into his place now. Rāvaṇa looked at the attendant and laughed at his face. Nandī answered that beings with a monkey's form would one day break the pride of Rāvaṇa and his family.

Rāvaṇa did not turn back. He put his many arms under Kailāsa. If the mountain blocked his chariot, he would move the mountain. He pushed upward, and the ground began to shake. Śiva's attendants trembled. Pārvatī held on to Śiva.

· · ·

Then Śiva pressed one toe against the mountain. Kailāsa came down on Rāvaṇa's arms. He could not pull them free. His cry ran through the three worlds. The strength that had lifted a mountain could not lift it now.

His ministers called to him: honour Śiva; seek his help. Caught beneath the mountain, Rāvaṇa recited hymns and sacred words. The telling gives this a vast span of time. He did not command the mountain again. He kept singing.

At last Śiva released his arms. He spoke of Rāvaṇa's courage and endurance, and gave him a name drawn from that great cry: Rāvaṇa. The king bowed, climbed back into Puṣpaka and went on his way.

The tale does not say that this meeting made every later act of Rāvaṇa right. It holds two things together: his fierce devotion and the pride that made him think another's home was his to move. At Kailāsa, a single toe was enough to make him stop and sing instead.

Long before Rāma reached Laṅkā, Rāvaṇa crossed the world in the flying chariot called Puṣpaka. He had taken it from his brother, the lord of wealth. Its great power seemed to answer his will: wherever he wished to travel, it carried him.

Then, near Mount Kailāsa, the chariot stopped. Rāvaṇa pressed on, but Puṣpaka would not move. He looked toward the mountain and wondered who had dared to stand in his way.

A small figure came to meet him. This was Nandī, an attendant of Śiva. Nandī told the king that Śiva was on the mountain and that no visitor, however mighty, could pass into his place now. It was a boundary, plainly spoken.

· · ·

Rāvaṇa stared at Nandī and laughed at his face. The attendant did not step aside. He told Rāvaṇa that one day beings with the form of monkeys would rise against his strength and break his pride. Rāvaṇa heard the warning and ignored it.

If the mountain would not let his chariot through, he said, he would uproot the mountain itself. He went to its foot, set his many arms beneath the rock and pushed. Kailāsa shook. Its heights moved. Śiva's attendants were frightened, and Pārvatī clung to Śiva as the ground shifted around them.

Śiva did not have to gather an army. He pressed the mountain with one great toe. Down came Kailāsa, pinning Rāvaṇa's arms beneath its weight. A moment earlier those arms had seemed strong enough to lift a home of gods. Now he could not pull them free.

Rāvaṇa cried out. The sound shook the three worlds. His ministers heard it and urged him to turn toward the one whose mountain he had tried to move. They told him to praise Śiva and seek release. The king could still roar, but a roar would not raise Kailāsa.

· · ·

So Rāvaṇa began to recite hymns and sacred words. The telling counts a thousand years of praise before he is freed. His arms could not move the stone, but his voice went on. He had demanded a path across Śiva's mountain. Now he asked the lord of that mountain to hear him.

At last Śiva was pleased. He lifted the weight from Rāvaṇa's arms and praised his courage and endurance. He also gave him a name that would stay with him. The cry that had made the worlds tremble would be remembered in the name Rāvaṇa.

Rāvaṇa then asked for a boon. Śiva gave him a shining sword. The king bowed, returned to Puṣpaka and continued across the earth. His path was not finished. The Rāmāyaṇa will tell of the harm he does, and of the people who stand against him. This meeting does not erase any of that.

· · ·

It is possible to be brave and devoted in one moment, and still need to answer for what one does in another. The scene does not ask us to pretend Rāvaṇa was only one thing. He could lift a mountain, mock the one who guarded it, and then spend an immense stretch of time praising the god who stopped him.

Nor does the story turn Nandī's warning into an empty threat. Later, the monkey allies of Rāma will matter greatly in the struggle with Rāvaṇa. Here the warning lands before any of them appear. The king has heard a future he refuses to imagine.

What he can feel now is the weight of Kailāsa and the limit of his own arms. The mountain was not an obstacle placed there for his journey. It was Śiva's dwelling, and Nandī had told him so. Rāvaṇa chose to treat another's boundary as something to tear away.

The toe presses down. The shouting changes to praise. Then the arms are freed. That is the turn this telling keeps in view: strength cannot make every place yours, and even a king who will later do wrong can be heard when he calls on Śiva with devotion.

After the story

Why did Rāvaṇa try to move the mountain instead of turning his chariot around?

He had come to expect every path to answer his strength, even a place someone else called home.

Did Rāvaṇa really sing for a thousand years?

Uttara Kāṇḍa 16 gives his praise that vast span; it marks how long and deeply he turned his voice toward Śiva. The telling does not give the hymns a surviving title.

Was Rāvaṇa good after Śiva let him go?

Śiva acknowledges his courage and devotion here. The Rāmāyaṇa also tells the harm Rāvaṇa later causes; this meeting does not cancel that.

Neither of Them Found the End · source ledger

Śiva Purāṇa, Vidyeśvara Saṃhitā · chapters 6–8

This follows the Vidyeśvara Saṃhitā, where Ketakī says it fell from the pillar’s middle and Śiva stops Bhairava before a blow. The Skanda Purāṇa has the flower claim an earlier place at the pillar’s crown; we keep the witnesses distinct.

  1. Brahmā and Viṣṇu dispute precedence and fight; their weapons threaten the worlds. Vidyeśvara Saṃhitā 6.1–22; 7.8–10
  2. Śiva appears as a limitless fire pillar that receives the weapons. 7.10–14
  3. Viṣṇu goes down as a boar, fails to find the root, and returns. 7.15–18
  4. Brahmā flies upward as a swan; the falling Ketakī flower says it came from the middle, not the top. 7.16–23
  5. Brahmā persuades Ketakī to testify falsely; Viṣṇu tells the truth about his failure, then honours Brahmā after the claim. 7.24–28
  6. Śiva emerges from the pillar and praises Viṣṇu for truthfulness. 7.29–33
  7. Bhairava threatens Brahmā’s fifth head, but Viṣṇu intercedes and Śiva stops him. 8.1–8
  8. Śiva denies Brahmā separate temples and festivals while retaining his role in sacrifices. 8.9–14
  9. Śiva excludes Ketakī from offerings but permits followers to wear it and its use above his image. 8.15–21

Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa · Bāla Kāṇḍa 1.40–44

This telling follows Vālmīki’s Bāla Kāṇḍa, including Gaṅgā’s confinement in Śiva’s hair and the interruption at Jahnu’s rite. Purāṇic accounts also tell her descent, but the scene sequence and claims here are from the Rāmāyaṇa.

  1. Sagara’s sixty thousand sons are reduced to ashes; Gaṅgā’s water is needed to release them. Bāla Kāṇḍa 1.40–41
  2. Aṃśumān and Dilīpa wish to bring Gaṅgā down but die without achieving it. 1.42.1–11; 1.44.9–12
  3. Bhagīratha delegates the kingdom, goes to Gokarṇa, and performs austerities. 1.42.10–15
  4. Bhagīratha asks Brahmā that Gaṅgā touch his ancestors’ ashes and that his line continue. 1.42.16–22
  5. Brahmā says only Śiva can bear Gaṅgā’s descent; Bhagīratha then prays to Śiva. 1.42.23–24; 1.43.1–4
  6. Śiva catches Gaṅgā in his matted hair, confines her, and releases her only after Bhagīratha renews his austerity. 1.43.5–12

  8. Gaṅgā floods Jahnu’s rite; he drinks her and later releases her from his ears, giving her the name Jāhnavī. 1.43.33–39
  9. Gaṅgā reaches the ocean, enters the lower world, touches the sons’ ashes, and they reach heaven. 1.43.39–41; 1.44.1–4
  10. Brahmā says Gaṅgā will bear Bhagīratha’s name; Bhagīratha performs offerings and returns to his city. 1.44.4–18

Two Lights in the Anthill · source ledger

Mahābhārata · Vana Parva, Tīrtha-yātrā Parva, sections CXXII–CXXIII in Ganguli's translation

We follow the Mahābhārata: Sukanyā identifies Cyavana among three identical young men. The Bhāgavata Purāṇa tells this differently: she cannot distinguish him, and the Aśvins show her which man is her husband.

  1. Cyavana performs long austerities beside a lake until an anthill covered with creepers grows over him. Vana CXXII, opening
  2. King Śaryāti visits the lake with his daughter Sukanyā; she sees two points of light, does not hear Cyavana's quiet call, and pierces his eyes with thorns. Vana CXXII, forest and anthill
  3. The sage's pain and anger afflict the king's troops by blocking their bodily functions; Sukanyā tells her father what she did. Vana CXXII, affliction and confession
  4. Cyavana requires Sukanyā as bride as the condition for forgiving the king; Śaryāti gives her to him, and she tends him and their sacred fire. Vana CXXII, settlement and marriage
  5. The divine physician twins, the Aśvins, ask Sukanyā to leave Cyavana; she refuses. They offer to make him young if she chooses among the three afterward. Vana CXXIII, encounter and offer
  6. Cyavana agrees to enter the lake; all three emerge young and alike. Sukanyā identifies Cyavana and chooses him. Vana CXXIII, lake and choice
  7. Cyavana promises to secure soma for the Aśvins in thanks, and they depart. Vana CXXIII, closing

The Jar Above the Cot · source ledger

Pañcatantra, Pūrṇabhadra recension in Arthur W. Ryder's translation · Book V, The Brahman's Dream (embedded tale following Slow, the Weaver)

This telling follows Ryder's Pūrṇabhadra text: the jar holds barley-meal and hangs above the brahmin's cot. Retellings that say rice or put the pot on a shelf are not the witness used here.

  1. A wheel-bearer tells a gold-finder the brahmin's dream as an example of extravagant hope. Book V, Slow, the Weaver, closing transition to The Brahman's Dream
  2. A poor brahmin obtains barley-meal by begging, eats some, fills a jar with the remainder, hangs it on a peg, and lies beneath it on a cot. Book V, The Brahman's Dream, opening
  3. He imagines selling the meal during a famine for one hundred rupees and buying two she-goats. The Brahman's Dream, imagined famine sale
  4. His imagined herd leads successively to cows, buffaloes, mares, horses, gold and a large house. The Brahman's Dream, imagined trading chain
  5. He imagines marriage, a son named Moon-Lord, reading by the stable, and the child approaching the horses. The Brahman's Dream, imagined household
  6. He imagines ordering his wife to fetch the child, her being busy, and himself kicking her; his actual kick smashes the jar and barley-meal covers him. The Brahman's Dream, final imagined action and actual loss
  7. The frame's warning concerns extravagant hopes and failing to consider consequences. Slow, the Weaver, transition; The Brahman's Dream, closing transition

When Rāvaṇa Shook Kailāsa · source ledger

Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Uttara Kāṇḍa · Uttara Kāṇḍa, sarga 16, the shaking of Kailāsa and the naming of Rāvaṇa

This follows Uttara Kāṇḍa 16, a later compositional layer but traditionally transmitted as part of Vālmīki’s Rāmāyaṇa. Its Nandī, toe-pinning and naming sequence differs from Śiva Purāṇa, Koṭirudra 28. Uttara 16 does not name the hymns as the extant Śiva Tāṇḍava Stotram.

  1. Puṣpaka stops at Kailāsa and Nandī bars passage. Uttara Kāṇḍa 16, opening encounter
  2. Rāvaṇa mocks Nandī, who foretells monkey-shaped beings undoing his pride. Uttara Kāṇḍa 16, Nandī encounter
  3. Rāvaṇa shakes Kailāsa; Pārvatī clings to Śiva; Śiva pins Rāvaṇa’s arms by pressing the mountain with one toe. Uttara Kāṇḍa 16, mountain-shaking sequence
  4. Rāvaṇa cries out, recites hymns and sacred words over a thousand years, and Śiva releases him and names him Rāvaṇa. Uttara Kāṇḍa 16, release and naming sequence
  5. Śiva gives Rāvaṇa a shining sword before he departs. Uttara Kāṇḍa 16, concluding boon

# Mudgala Asked What Heaven Would Cost — English editorial draft

Draft; human source/editorial and spoken review pending. Age 10+. Care: a demanding guest repeatedly eats the household's food; Mudgala goes hungry. His wife and son are in the account, but their feelings are not supplied. Do not use his adult vow as a rule that children must give up meals or accept mistreatment. Witness: Mahābhārata, Vana Parva, Ganguli sections 258–259. Heaven and liberation are distinct in this telling.

## Short — target 3 minutes

«Mudgala» gathered the grains left in the fields after harvest. He lived with his wife and son.

On the days of his offerings, guests ate before he did. The account says his food increased as guests arrived, enough to feed hundreds.

Then «Durvāsa» came. He spoke harshly and asked for food.

«Mudgala» welcomed him. The guest ate everything offered, asked for more, and smeared the leftovers on his body before leaving.

Six times, on successive feeding occasions, he came. «Mudgala» went hungry and gathered grain again. He did not turn against his guest.

At last «Durvāsa» praised him. He knew what hunger could do to patience.

«Mudgala» had kept his restraint. He would be able to go to heaven in his own body.

A messenger arrived with a heavenly car. Bells hung from it; swans and cranes drew it. The messenger invited «Mudgala» to enter.

«Mudgala» had a question first.

_What is happy about heaven? And what are its disadvantages? Tell me both, so I can choose._

The messenger described beautiful gardens. There was no hunger, thirst or weariness. Those who had earned that place enjoyed the results of their good deeds.

Then he answered the other half. While enjoying those results, they could not earn new merit there.

When their merit was exhausted, they had to leave. Before departure, their garlands faded, and fear came.

«Mudgala» considered all he had heard. He bowed to the messenger and declined.

He wanted a freedom that would not end in another fall. The messenger spoke of the supreme abode of «Viṣṇu», reached through freedom from pride and attachment, contemplation and self-restraint.

The messenger departed. «Mudgala» stayed, gathered grain, and continued his practice. Through meditation and knowledge, he reached liberation.

He had listened to the invitation. He had also listened to the answer nobody offered until he asked.

## Full — target 6 minutes

After the harvest, when the cut grain had been carried away, «Mudgala» gathered what remained in the fields. There were fallen ears to pick up and loose grains to collect. That was how he lived at «Kurukṣetra», with his wife and son.

He gathered a measure of grain over a fortnight. At the new moon and full moon, he made his offerings and welcomed guests. He ate what was left after the offerings and the visitors' meals.

His small supply did something remarkable. When a guest arrived, the food increased. The account tells of hundreds of learned brāhmaṇas being fed.

«Indra» and other devas came to receive their portions at his offerings. «Mudgala» served with a cheerful heart.

«Durvāsa» heard about this man and his vows. He came to «Mudgala», speaking harshly and asking for food. «Mudgala» welcomed him, offered water for washing, and placed a meal before him.

The visitor ate it all. «Mudgala» supplied more.

That too was eaten. Before leaving, the guest smeared leftovers on his own body.

When the next feeding occasion came, «Durvāsa» returned. Again he ate all that «Mudgala» supplied.

The host had no meal himself. He went back to gathering the grains left in the fields.

This happened six times. Hunger did not turn «Mudgala» angry.

Neither insult nor the loss of his food unsettled his mind. He continued his vow.

After the sixth visit, «Durvāsa» spoke differently. He praised «Mudgala» for generosity and restraint. Hunger could drive patience away, he said.

Food sustained life. Giving up something earned through such effort was hard. Yet «Mudgala» had done it without anger or deceit.

He declared that «Mudgala» could go to heaven in his own body.

While he was speaking, a messenger of the devas appeared. He had brought a heavenly car, drawn by swans and cranes.

Bells decorated it. It carried a divine fragrance and could travel wherever it wished.

The messenger invited «Mudgala» to ascend. This was the result his deeds had earned.

But «Mudgala» did not enter. He asked about the place to which he was being invited.

_What happiness is there? What do its people do?_

_And what disadvantages does it have? Tell me the truth, so I can decide._

He spoke of a friendship formed by walking seven steps together. In the name of that friendship, he asked the messenger to hold nothing back. Once he knew the whole account, he would choose his own course.

The messenger described bright worlds and beautiful gardens. There was no hunger, thirst or weariness.

Pleasant scents and sounds filled those regions. Those who had earned them lived there through the merit of their own deeds.

He described their shining forms, their fragrant garlands, and heavenly cars like the one waiting before «Mudgala». There were higher regions too, each with its own blessings.

Then the messenger came to the disadvantages.

In those worlds, people enjoyed the results of deeds already performed. They could not perform new deeds to add to that merit there. Once it was exhausted, their stay ended.

Before someone fell from heaven, the garland began to fade. Fear disturbed the person who had grown used to happiness.

Leaving a brighter place for a lower one brought regret. Enjoyment had not freed that person from another return.

«Mudgala» thought carefully. Then he bowed to the messenger.

_Go in peace. I do not desire this heaven. I want the place from which people do not return to sorrow._

The messenger spoke of the supreme abode of «Viṣṇu», pure and eternal. Attachment, pride, anger and envy did not lead there. Contemplation and self-restraint were part of that path.

«Mudgala» said farewell. He continued gathering grain.

Praise and blame ceased to pull him in opposite directions. He practised meditation, gained knowledge, and attained liberation.

The beautiful car had come to take him away. One careful question had helped him choose where he wanted to go.

## Parent-facing close and questions

- Open question: What would you want to know before saying yes to a wonderful invitation?
- Seed, only if needed: A good choice needs the whole answer.
- If they ask, “Was the guest being fair?”: His visits are severe: he consumes the food and leaves the host hungry. The account presents this as a test of an adult ascetic's vow. It does not tell us the wife or son's thoughts. We need not invent their approval, or use this episode to tell a child to accept hunger or harsh treatment.
- If they ask, “Is heaven bad?”: The messenger describes real blessings in the source's terms. He also says that the stay earned by deeds ends when their merit is exhausted. Mudgala seeks liberation beyond that cycle. He is choosing his spiritual aim, not belittling heaven or everyone who desires it.
- If they ask, “Did the car take him away?”: No. After hearing both sides, he respectfully declines. The messenger leaves. Mudgala continues his practice and later reaches liberation through meditation and knowledge. A version that puts him in the car changes the ending.
